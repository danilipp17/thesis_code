\chapter{Introduction}

Over the past several years, agentic AI systems have moved from research prototypes to real world production deployments, driven by organizational demand to automate complex tasks without continuous human oversight \cite{fi17090404}. In contrast to conventional AI systems, agentic systems are capable of planning, delegating and acting autonomously across extended task  \cite{deepaketal2025}. However, as these systems scale to handle more sophisticated and distributed workflows, their underlying architectures become increasingly complex, exposing severe challenges in interoperability, scalability as well as system integration \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures}. 

This is particularly critical as agentic AI systems, in practice, are frequently developed in a monolithic and ad hoc manner, relying on framework-specific abstractions and tightly coupled implementations \cite{Sapkota_2026, ekelhart2026agento}. Frameworks such as LangGraph, CrewAI, and AutoGen each realize similar agentic concepts through fundamentally different code-level constructs (e.g. graph-based state machines, role-based agent teams, and conversation-driven orchestration). Architectural decisions and coordination logic are often hard-coded into source code, thereby limiting modularity and transferability across systems \cite{ekelhart2026agento}. This lack of standardized and transferable architectural semantics has been identified as a central challenge for agentic AI, as it hinders the comparison of implementations, the replication of results, and generalization across domains \cite{Sapkota_2026}.

Existing semantic representations partially address these challenges by providing expressive conceptual vocabularies for agents, tasks and workflows. More recent work such as AgentO \cite{ekelhart2026agento} additionally demonstrates the feasibility of extracting agentic workflows from framework-specific source code into a unified ontology. However, these approaches remain focused on unidirectional extraction and do not model constructs such as conditional control flow, state management, or prompt-level semantics at sufficient depth to support bidirectional transformation between frameworks. This unidirectional limitation shows that existing representations are not expressive enough to serve as a shared intermediate layer between frameworks and therefore cannot support the systematic migration or comparison of agentic architectures across different implementations. 

This thesis aims to contribute to addressing the challenges above and enrich the discourse on the topic of Agentic AI Systems and their semantic presentation. Specifically, we aim to answer the main research question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{To what extent can semantic representations enable interoperability between agentic AI frameworks?}
\end{quote}

Answering this question requires demonstrating that the vocabulary is rich enough to carry the architectural information of real agentic systems in both directions. This means from framework-specific implementations into a formal representation, and from that representation back into executable code. The thesis approaches the question through round-trip transformation, using the fidelity of extraction and regeneration as a measurable indicator of how far a shared ontology can close the gap between frameworks. To structure this research, the main question is split into four sub-questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Which architectural constructs must a semantic representation support to capture agentic AI systems implemented in CrewAI, AutoGen and LangGraph?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} To what extent can semantic representations of agentic AI systems be systematically derived from their source code?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent can the source code of agentic AI systems be generated from their semantic representations?
\item \textbf{RQ4:} Which methods can be used to adequately evaluate the quality of extraction and generation of agentic AI systems from framework-specific source code?
\end{itemize}

RQ1 establishes the conceptual foundation by identifying what a shared representation must express to capture the constructs found in contemporary agentic AI frameworks. The outcome of this phase is a list of requirements a semantic representation needs to fulfill, to function as a framework-agnostic semantic representation. This list also acts as the guideline of the subsequent ontology evaluation, where we will check whether our ontology provides a higher concepts coverage than existing data models. 

RQ2 and RQ3 form the two halves of the round-trip: the first concerns lifting framework-specific implementations into the semantic representation, the second concerns regenerating executable implementations from it. RQ4 addresses how the results of both directions can be evaluated rigorously rather than asserted, and grounds the evaluation through comparison against a large language model-driven baseline.

\chapter{Related Work}

This chapter reviews prior work relevant to the thesis in three areas: semantic representations of agentic and AI systems, the landscape of contemporary multi-agent frameworks, and model transformation approaches that bridge source code and semantic models.

\section{Semantic Representations for Agentic and AI Systems}

Alongside the development of agentic AI frameworks, several semantic representations have been proposed to capture the structure and interactions of complex AI systems.
The BEAM ontology provides a high-level semantic model for representing AI systems within their operational and organizational context \cite{Ekaputra2025}. BEAM is an extension of the orginial Boxology \cite{DBLP} and focuses on systems, resources and contextual elements, enabling structured documentation of AI-based solutions and their deployment environments. While BEAM supports architectural description and contextual reasoning, it remains largely agnostic to fine-grained agentic control structures and execution semantics, which limits its suitability for detailed modeling of multi-agent workflows.

AgentO is a recent ontology designed specifically for agentic AI systems and is the primary semantic foundation on which the present work builds \cite{ekelhart2026agento}. Its central premise is that contemporary agentic frameworks lack a shared conceptual model: each framework encodes its logic directly in code, resulting in monolithic implementations that hinder reusability, interoperability and auditing across systems. AgentO addresses this by offering a standardized RDF/OWL vocabulary for the recurring concepts of the agentic paradigm (agents, goals, tasks, tools, resources, workflows and their interrelations) so that agentic systems can be represented declaratively and independently of any particular framework.\cite{ekelhart2026agento} also developed an LLM-driven extraction pipeline that transforms source code from four agentic frameworks (AutoGen, CrewAI, LangGraph and Mastra AI) into instances of the ontology, producing a knowledge graph of 66 agentic patterns. The authors demonstrate the utility of this representation through three use cases: declarative reconstruction of agentic workflows, reuse of agent and task configurations across problem contexts, and auditing of agentic implementations for transparency and traceability \cite{ekelhart2026agento}.

\section{Multi-agent Systems and Frameworks}

Multi-agent systems are not a new research field. Work on coordinated autonomous agents dates back  decades \cite{wooldridge-1995, wooldridge-2009}. Classical MAS research was driven by questions of distributed problem solving, concurrent rationality and open systems. What large language models have changed is not the underlying paradigm but the foucs. Agents are now natural-language reasoners with broad general competence rather than narrow symbolic planners, and the coordination problem has accordingly shifted from symbolic message exchange to prompt engineering, tool orchestration and workflow control \cite{botti2025agenticaimultiagenticreinventing, guo2024largelanguagemodelbased}. 

The transition towards LLM-based multi-agent systems was motivated in large part by tasks that exceed the context window, domain coverage or reasoning budget of any single model. Early LLM-based frameworks explored different communication topologies to manage this coordination, broadly categorizing interactions into chain (waterfall), star (hub-and-spoke), and mesh (swarm) patterns\cite{arunkumar2026}. Among these three agentic AI frameworks have become particularly influential in both research and production settings: AutoGen, CrewAI and LangGraph \cite{botti2025agenticaimultiagenticreinventing}. Each realizes multi-agent collaboration through a different architectural commitment.

AutoGen treats multi-agent collaboration as a conversation between conversable entities \cite{wu2023autogenenablingnextgenllm}. Its underlying model is dialogical: agents, tools and humans are all first-class participants in a shared message stream, and the system's behavior emerges from the rules that govern who speaks next. CrewAI treats multi-agent collaboration as role-based teamwork. Agents are defined by personas and responsibilities, organized into crews, and coordinated through processes that resemble organizational structures\cite{crewai_docs_2025}. LangGraph, developed by LangChain, treats multi-agent collaboration as a programmable workflow. Its underlying model is computational: the system is a state machine whose transitions are explicit and whose execution is traceable. Agent identity is secondary to graph structure, which places LangGraph closer to workflow engines and flow-based programming than to either dialogue systems or role-based teams \cite{langgraph_docs_2025, clark-2026}.

\section{Model-based transformation}

In the context of agentic AI systems, recent work such as AgentO demonstrates the feasibility of extracting agentic workflows from framework specific implementations into a unified semantic representation using Large Language Models \cite{ekelhart2026agento}. These approaches enable declarative reconstruction, cross framework comparison, and workflow auditing. However, they remain focused on one directional extraction and analysis and do not provide mechanisms for regenerating framework specific implementations from semantic models.   

Model Driven Engineering (MDE) provides an established foundation for closing this gap. In MDE, models are treated as primary development artefacts from which executable code is generated rather than as mere documentation \cite{MDEMobile, MDESoftware}. The paradigm has been applied to many domains but not, so far, to agentic AI frameworks. A concrete instantiation relevant to the present work is the Ontology-based Source Code Interoperability (OSCIN) method \cite{oscin}. OSCIN separates syntactic from semantic abstraction. Source code is first parsed into a syntactic structure specific to its host language, and then mapped to a shared ontology representing a common subdomain. By routing heterogeneous source languages through a central operational ontology, this method mitigates both syntactic and semantic heterogeneity and exposes a single abstraction layer to downstream tools.

However, similar to extraction method developed by AgentO \cite{ekelhart2026agento}, OSCIN focuses primarily on interoperability and perspective-based analysis rather than the feasiblity of bidirectional transformation. While it successfully abstracts source code into rich, queryable semantic graphs that can unify different languages under a common domain consensus, it serves as an analytical framework and does not natively provide mechanisms to dynamically regenerate executable, framework-specific implementations from a semantic representation.

\section{Research Gap}

The research gap this thesis addresses is whether a shared ontology can represent agentic AI systems with enough details that framework-specific implementations can be extracted into the ontology and regenerated from it, and how much semantic information is lost in each direction. Existing work on AgentO has shown that extraction alone is possible, but the inverse direction has not been attempted, and without it the adequacy of the representation cannot be rigorously tested. Round-trip transformation therefore serves as both the method and the measurement. It forces the ontology to carry enough structure to reconstruct executable systems, and the fidelity loss it exposes becomes a direct indicator of where the shared representation succeeds and where it reaches its limits.

This research follows the Design Science Research (DSR) approach as defined by \cite{hevneretal2004} and \cite{hevner2007}. Requirement elicitation addresses the \emph{relevance cycle}, ontology engineering bridges the \emph{rigor} and \emph{design cycles}, and the bidirectional transformation method together with its evaluation completes the \emph{design cycle}.

\section{Requirement Elicitation through Literature Review and Framework Analysis}

The requirement elicitation phase aims to identify representational requirements for a semantic model of agentic AI systems that supports bidirectional transformation. The phase is structured in two steps.

The first step is a literature review conducted according to \cite{grantandbooth}. The review follows a structured process of identifying relevant sources, extracting recurring architectural concepts and organizing them into a cross-paper concept matrix (see Chapter~\ref{chap:requirements}). The search string ("agentic AI" OR "multi-agent systems") AND ("ontology" OR "representation" OR "architecture") was applied to Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar with a cut-off date of April 20, 2026. 

Because the research field is new and rapidly evolving, preprints were included alongside peer-reviewed publications. The initial result set of around 220 papers was extended through backward snowballing: references cited in the papers retained after full-text screening were examined and included when they satisfied the same selection criteria. The results were screened in three stages: title, abstract and full text. A paper was included if it (i) addressed the architectural design of LLM-based or classical multi-agent systems, (ii) was written in English and (iii) was accessible in full text. A paper was excluded if it (i) focused on single-agent systems without multi-agent considerations, (ii) treated multi-agent systems purely as an application case study rather than analyzing their design, (iii) concentrated on agent communication protocols or runtime behavior rather than on architectural structure. Classical multi-agent systems references such as Wooldridge's foundational work~\cite{wooldridge-1995, wooldridge-2009} were included as conceptual anchor points for the taxonomy.

The retained papers were then analyzed to extract the architectural concepts each paper identifies as constitutive of agentic AI systems. Concepts were coded individually per paper and afterwards aggregated across the corpus. Semantically related concepts were grouped into higher-level categories, which form the main concepts of the taxonomy. Finer-grained concepts that refine or specialize a main concept are retained as sub concepts beneath it. The result is a two-tier concept matrix that records, for each paper in the corpus, which main concepts and sub concepts it addresses.

The second step is an analysis of framework-specific artifacts. The main-concepts identified in the literature review are examined at the source code level across three agentic AI frameworks that represent fundamentally different architectural paradigms: sequential role-based (CrewAI), graph-based (LangGraph) and conversation-driven (AutoGen). For each concept, the analysis documents how, and whether, the concept is realized in each framework. Developer documentation~\cite{crewai_docs_2025, autogen_docs_2025, langgraph_docs_2025} and selected open-source code bases of the three frameworks are the primary sources. The specific versions analyzed are CrewAI 1.13.0, AutoGen (\texttt{autogen-agentchat}) 0.7.5, and LangGraph at the state of its main branch as of April 20, 2026. The output of this step is a set of requirements that a framework-agnostic ontology must fulfill in order to represent agentic AI systems across the three target frameworks. Each requirement is traceable back to at least one framework-level construct, one literature concept or both, ensuring that the requirement set is scientifically grounded.

\section{Ontology Engineering using Ontology Development 101}

The second methodological step is the development of a formal semantic representation for agentic AI systems. This thesis proposes the development of a framework-agnostic ontology to semantically represent the requirements of phase one. Ontology engineering is conducted following the Ontology Development 101 methodology~\cite{onto101} and applied in four iterative activities.

First, the scope and domain of the ontology are determined based on the concepts and requirements list produced in the previous phase. The scope is bounded by the three target frameworks and the agentic AI concepts identified across them. Second, competency questions are formulated to define what the ontology must be able to answer. These questions are derived directly from the requirements elicitation and serve both as design guidance during modeling and as a validation criterion during evaluation. Third, existing vocabularies are evaluated for reuse. AgentO~\cite{ekelhart2026agento} is adopted as the primary starting point because it already covers the recurring concepts of the agentic paradigm and builds on established upper vocabularies (PROV-O, P-Plan and BEAM). Additional data models (e.g. unified class model by \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures}) are also analyzed to gather additional vocabulary for our ontology.  

Concepts or properties that are not expressible by existing literature, as identified through the requirement analysis, are introduced as AgentO extensions rather than as a parallel vocabulary. This includes constructs required for bidirectional transformation. Fourth, the extended ontology is formalized in OWL to ensure machine interpretability and compatibility with existing Semantic Web standards. The resulting ontology is referred to as AgentOSCIN.

\section{Bidirectional Transformation using the OSCIN Method}

The third methodological step is the construction of a bidirectional transformation method that links framework-specific source code of agentic AI systems to AgentOSCIN. The method follows the five phases of the OSCIN method for ontology-based source code interoperability.

The first phase (i.e. specification) defines the scope of the transformation. The subdomain is agentic AI systems, the source language is Python, and the target frameworks are CrewAI, LangGraph and AutoGen. The second phase, Subdomain Semantic Abstraction, is the construction of the domain ontology and is addressed by the ontology engineering step above. The third phase, Language Syntactic Abstraction, defines how source code is parsed into syntactic structures that the transformation can operate on. The fourth phase, Language Semantic Abstraction, maps these syntactic structures to the corresponding instances of our semantic representation (i.e. AgentOSCIN) \cite{oscin}.

The fifth phase, Application Perspective, specifies what is done with the populated ontology. The original OSCIN method applies this phase to analytical operations over the semantic graph such as querying, comparison and auditing \cite{oscin}. This thesis extends the fifth phase with code generation: the populated ontology is treated not only as an analytical artefact but also as a source from which framework-specific source code can be regenerated. This extension is what makes the transformation bidirectional and is one of the contributions of this thesis.

The transformation pipeline is deterministic. It operates through static parsing and mapping rules. Constructs that cannot be resolved without interpretation of natural-language content or semantic analysis of source code are identified but excluded from the pipeline, in order to preserve reproducibility. The resulting gaps in coverage are reported as part of the evaluation and discussed as limitations and directions for future work. The technical realization of the pipeline, including parser implementation, intermediate data structures and code generation, is presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:transformation}.

\section{Evaluation}

The evaluation is structured along two complementary dimensions, reflecting the interdependent nature of both research artifacts (i.e., AgentOSCIN and the corresponding transformation method).

\subsubsection{Ontology Evaluation}

The first evaluation dimension assesses the quality of AgentOSCIN as a semantic representation for agentic AI systems. The evaluation examines whether the ontology captures enough of the architectural concepts required to support cross-framework representation, following the validation approach outlined in Ontology Development 101~\cite{onto101} and extending it with a comparative baseline.
AgentOSCIN is evaluated by checking, for each competency question defined during ontology engineering, whether the question can be answered from the structure the ontology provides.

To assess whether the proposed extensions add more expressiveness beyond existing representations, the same competency questions are evaluated against comparable data models from the literature analyzed in chapter 4. AgentO~\cite{ekelhart2026agento} serves as the primary baseline, as it is the only existing ontology designed specifically for agentic AI systems and an direct starting point for AgentOSCIN. Coverage is classified on a three-level metric: \emph{full} when all concepts required to answer the question are representable in the model, \emph{partial} when some but not all required concepts are representable, and \emph{none} when the model provides no means of representing the concepts the copmpetency question asks about. The resulting coverage profiles are compared across the models to quantify the expressiveness gain contributed by AgentOSCIN.

\subsubsection{Transformation Experiments}

The second evaluation dimension uses controlled transformation experiments to assess the operational usability of the ontology and the OSCIN-based transformation method.

In the \emph{extraction direction}, framework-specific source code is transformed into instances of the semantic representation. The resulting ABox assertions are evaluated against the original source code with respect to \emph{transformation correctness} (the degree to which agentic AI concepts are accurately captured) and \emph{transformation completeness} (the coverage of architectural elements such as agents, coordination structures, control flow and tool usage). Evaluation is performed by identifying the structural patterns through which agentic concepts are defined in each framework, including agent instantiation and workflow composition, and assessing whether these patterns are faithfully reflected in the populated ABox.

In the \emph{generation direction}, ontology instances are transformed into executable source code for a target framework. Correctness is evaluated by examining whether the generated code complies with the structural requirements of the target framework and reproduces the intended system architecture. Structural consistency is assessed by comparing the generated code against reference implementations where available. The basis for generation will be the populated ontologies created in the extraction step and the generated source code will therefore be compared against the original source code. AST comparisons will serve as the structural metric while runtime execution metrics will be calculated to assess the functionality of the generated source code. Here, the count of errors, manual corrections and differences in runtime execution will constitute the primary evaluation metrics.

To evaluate \emph{cross-framework interoperability}, transformation experiments are conducted across all three frameworks. Source code from one framework is transformed into the semantic representation, regenerated into a second framework and then re-extracted into an ontology instance (see Figure~\ref{fig:roundtrip}). The original and reconstructed ontology instances are compared using structural equivalence criteria such as the preservation of agent roles, interaction patterns and control structures.

\begin{figure}[h] \centering \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{roundtrip.png} \caption{Cross-framework transformation experiment linking source code, semantic representation and regenerated target-framework code.} \label{fig:roundtrip} \end{figure}

Across all experiments, the proposed approach is compared to an LLM-based baseline that uses large language models for direct code-to-model and model-to-code translation without formal semantic mappings. Comparison criteria include correctness, completeness, structural equivalence, consistency across transformation directions and reproducibility (whether the same input produces consistent outputs across repeated executions).

Together, the ontology evaluation and the transformation experiments provide the empirical basis for answering RQ4 by combining ontology-level quality assessment with evaluation of extraction and generation across heterogeneous frameworks.

Although their work was written before the current rise of generative AI, it still provides a solid foundation for understanding how modern agentic systems are designed. Recent studies have updated these classical ideas to fit LLM-based agents (e.g. \cite{ekelhart2026agento, derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures, botti2025agenticaimultiagenticreinventing}. This includes adding new elements like tool use, memory architectures and reasoning patterns.

\section{Agent Definition}

\cite{wooldridge-1995} provide one of the earliest comprehensive surveys of agent theories, architectures and languages, establishing core properties such as autonomy, reactivity, proactivity and social ability as defining characteristics of an agent. They define agents as a computing system situated in an environment that is capable of flexible, autonomous action to meet its design objectives. 

In the context of modern LLM-based multi-agent systems, the agent remains a central architectural component, but its characterization has changed from simple reactive/proactive agents to fully self-aware agents, that exhibit meta-cognitve awareness \cite{NISA202669}. 

In the context of the modern AI landscape, an agent is based on a foundation model (like a Large Language Model) and pursues complex, multi-step goals with limited direct supervision \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}. Additionally, these agents are often defined with specific profiles, roles, backgrounds and constraints that specify how they act within the agentic AI system \cite{lietal2024} \cite{guo2024largelanguagemodelbased}. 

\subsection{Framework Analysis}

\subsubsection{CrewAI}

CrewAI defines an Agent as an autonomous unit capable of performing tasks, making decisions, using tools, interacting with other agents, maintaining memory of interactions and being able to delegate tasks when allowed. In the source code, agents are defined either using YAML configuration or directly in code. To define an agent's profile, CrewAI provides a list of agent attributes that can be specified when designing an agentic system. The only required parameters are {role}, {goal} and {backstory} \cite{crewai_docs_2025}.


\begin{lstlisting}[language=yaml, caption={CrewAI agent definition (YAML configuration).}, label={lst:crewai-agent}]
data_analyst:
  role: Data Analyst
  goal: >
    Extract actionable insights from structured datasets
    and surface relevant patterns to the research team.
  backstory: >
    A seasoned analyst with expertise in statistical methods
    and business intelligence who prioritises clarity over
    complexity when communicating findings.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{AutoGen}

AutoGen provides a set of preset agent types that all inherit from the {BaseChatAgent} class. All agents share a common set of attributes that define their identity: {name}, {description} and {system\_message}, which are configured at agent instantiation. The {name} serves as a unique identifier within a multi-agent system the {description} communicates the agent's purpose to other agents during orchestration and the {system\_message} provides the behavioral instructions passed to the underlying language model.

Among the preset agents, {AssistantAgent} serves as a general-purpose agent intended for prototyping and educational use. For more specialized requirements, AutoGen allows developers to define custom agents by subclassing {BaseChatAgent}, enabling the implementation of domain-specific behavior that does not fit the provided presets \cite{autogen_docs_2025}.



\subsubsection{LangGraph}

LangGraph, while part of the LangChain ecosystem, follows a fundamentally different paradigm at the source code level than LangChain's agent abstractions. LangGraph explicitly distinguishes between \textit{workflows} and \textit{agents}: workflows follow predetermined code paths with a fixed execution order, whereas agents are driven by a language model that dynamically determines its own sequence of actions, typically through iterative tool use.

LangGraph does not provide its users with a mechanism to configure specific agents with roles, descriptions, or backstories. Instead, behavior is constructed using discrete steps called \textit{nodes}, connected via \textit{edges} that define transitions and conditional branching. An agent in LangGraph is therefore not defined through identity parameters but rather emerges as a node in which a language model autonomously decides whether to invoke tools or return a final response, typically implemented through a {react-style} loop \cite{langgraph_docs_2025}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=langgraph, caption={LangGraph agent definition as a node function}, label={lst:langgraph-agent}]

def data_analyst(state: MessagesState):
    system = SystemMessage(content=(
        "You are a data analyst with expertise in statistical "
        "methods and business intelligence. Prioritise clarity "
        "over complexity when communicating findings."
    ))
    response = model.invoke([system] + state["messages"])
    return {"messages": [response]}

\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Synthesis and Requirements}

The framework analysis of agent definitions revealed structural inconsistencies on an architectural level across all three frameworks. While CrewAI and AutoGen support defining individual agents with distinct characteristics and roles through dedicated parameters, LangGraph follows a fundamentally different approach in which agents are not explicitly declared but emerge from graph nodes that contain language model calls with tool access.

Despite these differences, all three frameworks share the underlying concept of an autonomous unit that is associated with a language model and guided by natural language instructions. CrewAI expresses this through {role}, {goal} and {backstory}; AutoGen through {name}, {description} and {system\_message} and LangGraph implicitly through prompt content within node functions.

From this analysis, the following representational requirements for agent definitions can be derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{R1}] \textbf{Framework-independent agent identity.} The ontology must represent agent identity as a separable concept that can be populated either from explicit configuration attributes (as in CrewAI and AutoGen) or inferred from prompt-level content within computational nodes (as in LangGraph).

    \item[\textbf{R2}] \textbf{Distinction between orchestration-relevant and model-relevant attributes.} The ontology must distinguish between agent attributes that serve the orchestration layer (e.g., AutoGen's description used for speaker selection) and those that instruct the language model (e.g., AutoGen's system\_message, CrewAI's backstory). A single agent may carry both types of directive simultaneously.

    \item[\textbf{R3}] \textbf{Prompt decomposition into structured components} The ontology must decompose natural language prompt content into structured components so that equivalent prompts can be reconstructed for different target frameworks from these components.

    \item[\textbf{R4}] \textbf{Flexible attribute cardinality.} The required and optional attributes differ across frameworks. CrewAI mandates {role}, {goal} and {backstory}; AutoGen requires only {name}; LangGraph has no required agent-level attributes at all. The ontology must define a minimal set of identity properties while accommodating framework-specific extensions without loss of information.

    \item[\textbf{R5}] \textbf{Agent functional typing.} The ontology must represent the functional role an agent plays within the coordination structure by distinguishing between general-purpose agents, manager/orchestrator agents, and agents that serve as proxies for human input (independently of how this role is expressed in framework-specific class hierarchies).
    
\end{enumerate}


\section{Perception Layer and Task Definition}

The perception layer is responsible for ingesting external inputs from the environment and translating them into internal representations that the agent can reason about \cite{fi17090404}. While early foundation models relied almost exclusively on text, modern agentic systems are increasingly equipped with multi-modal perception capabilities, processing inputs including textual descriptions, visual images, auditory signals and spatial coordinates \cite{lietal2024} \cite{wissuchek2025exploringagenticartificialintelligence}. 

Critically, agentic systems do not rely on rigid, explicit commands. They act upon abstract, high-level user goals. The perception layer translates these goals, alongside multi-modal context, into structured internal representations that drive the agent's behavior \cite{fi17090404}. It therefore constitutes the boundary at which human intent enters the agent system and takes on a computationally tractable form.

In the broader agentic AI literature, the perception layer describes how agents ingest and internalize external input at runtime. At the design-time abstraction level addressed by this thesis, the concern is how developers specify the tasks and actions an agent system should perform \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures}. The following analysis examines how the three frameworks define tasks, assign them to agents and express dependencies and output expectations.

\subsection{Framework Analysis}

\subsubsection{CrewAI}

CrewAI lets developers define a {Task} as a specific assignment completed by an agent. Similar to the {Agent} , tasks can be defined as YAML  configuration or directly in the code.

On a source code level, a task is created with two required parameters: {description}, a natural language statement of what the task entails and {expected\_output}, a description of what the completed task should produce. Tasks are optionally assigned to a specific agent via the {agent} parameter; if no agent is assigned, a hierarchical process can delegate tasks based on agent roles.

Tasks can depend on other tasks through the {context} attribute, which accepts a list of tasks whose outputs should be available as input. CrewAI also supports task-level tool assignment, allowing a task to override the agent's default tools, as well as output formatting through structured types such as {output\_pydantic} and {output\_json} \cite{crewai_docs_2025}.


\subsubsection{AutoGen}

In AutoGen, there is no dedicated task class or configuration object. Instead, a task is expressed as a message passed to an agent or team at through the {run} or {run\_stream} methods via the {task} parameter, which accepts a plain string. The task description, expected output and any constraints must all be encoded within this single string. There is no built-in mechanism for defining task dependencies, structured output formats, or guardrails at the task level; such logic must be implemented through agent orchestration or custom code \cite{autogen_docs_2025}.


\subsubsection{LangGraph}

LangGraph does not have an explicit task concept. What other frameworks model as a task is instead encoded in the graph structure itself: a node's function body contains the instructions (typically as part of the prompt passed to the language model) and the graph's edges define the execution order and dependencies between steps. Task descriptions, expected outputs and constraints are embedded within node implementations rather than declared as separate, configurable objects \cite{langgraph_docs_2025}.


\subsection{Synthesis and Requirements}

The framework analysis of task definitions revealed three distinct abstraction levels. CrewAI provides tasks as first-class, richly configurable objects with dedicated attributes for descriptions, expected outputs, agent assignment, dependencies, guardrails and structured output types. AutoGen reduces a task to a plain string passed at runtime, with no structural separation between the task description and its constraints. LangGraph has no explicit task concept; task logic is distributed across node functions and graph edges.

From this analysis, the following representational requirements can be derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{R6}] \textbf{Task as a first-class ontological concept.} The ontology must represent tasks as distinct entities with their own properties, even when the source framework does not model them explicitly. For AutoGen, this requires extracting task semantics from run method; for LangGraph, from node function bodies and associated prompts.

    \item[\textbf{R7}] \textbf{Agent-task assignment.} The ontology must represent the relationship between a task and the agent responsible for its execution. This includes explicit assignment (CrewAI's {agent} parameter), implicit assignment through orchestration (AutoGen's team routing) and structural assignment through graph topology (LangGraph's node-to-function binding).

    \item[\textbf{R8}] \textbf{Data dependencies.} The ontology must capture ordering and data dependencies between tasks. CrewAI models these explicitly through the {context} attribute, LangGraph encodes them as graph edges and AutoGen relies on sequential team execution or manual orchestration. The semantic representation must normalize these into a unified dependency model.

    \item[\textbf{R9}] \textbf{Expected output.} The ontology must represent the expected output of a task, both as a natural language description and, where available, as a structured schema definition. 
\end{enumerate}


\section{Coordination Mechanism	}

When tasks exceed the capacity of a single agent, Agentic AI employs MAS, where specialized agents coordinate their efforts to solve complex problems \cite{botti2025agenticaimultiagenticreinventing}. The architecture of this coordination typically falls into several structural categories: centralized orchestration, where a single supervisor agent decomposes tasks and routes them to sub-agents; hierarchical structures, where agents operate in a tree-like command flow; and decentralized or peer-to-peer networks, where agents negotiate and collaborate directly without a central authority \cite{fi17090404} \cite{lietal2024}.

Beyond structural organization, the mechanisms of coordination are defined by the interaction paradigms among agents, which can be cooperative, adversarial (debate), or mixed  \cite{lietal2024}. In cooperative teamwork, agents share information and divide labor to reach a common goal. Conversely, adversarial debate involves agents presenting and defending conflicting viewpoints to cross-validate reasoning, reduce hallucinations and refine solutions \cite{guo2024largelanguagemodelbased}. To efficiently manage the flow of information during these collaborative processes, systems often utilize a Shared Message Pool, a publish-subscribe mechanism allowing agents to extract only the information relevant to their specific roles without requiring direct point-to-point communication \cite{hong2024metagptmetaprogrammingmultiagent}.


\subsection{Framework Analysis}

\subsubsection{CrewAI}

CrewAI implements coordination at two distinct levels: \textit{Crews} for organizing agents around a shared set of tasks and \textit{Flows} for orchestrating multiple crews and coding tasks into larger workflows.

A Crew is a team of agents assigned to a list of tasks, governed by a \textit{process} that determines execution order. CrewAI provides two process types: {sequential}, where tasks are executed in the order they are defined with the output of one task serving as context for the next and {hierarchical}, where a manager agent dynamically delegates tasks to other agents based on their roles and capabilities. The process type is set at crew creation via the {process} parameter. In hierarchical mode, either a {manager\_llm} or a custom {manager\_agent} must be specified.

Flows operate at a higher abstraction level, connecting multiple crews, individual agents and arbitrary Python functions into event-driven workflows. A Flow is defined as a Python class inheriting from {Flow}, with methods decorated as entry points ({@start}), listeners ({@listen}), or routers ({@router}). Listeners are triggered when a specified method completes, enabling sequential chaining. Conditional logic is supported through {or\_} (trigger when any dependency completes), {and\_} (trigger when all dependencies complete) and router methods that return string labels to direct execution to different branches. Flows maintain their own state, which can be either unstructured (dictionary) or structured (Pydantic model) and is accessible across all methods in the flow \cite{crewai_docs_2025}.


\subsubsection{AutoGen}

AutoGen implements coordination through \textit{teams}, which are groups of agents that share a conversation context and take turns responding according to a defined interaction pattern. A team is instantiated by passing a list of agents and a termination condition that determines when execution stops.

AutoGen provides several team presets, each implementing a different speaker selection strategy:

\begin{itemize}
    \item {RoundRobinGroupChat}: Agents take turns in a fixed round-robin order. Each agent broadcasts its response to all other agents, maintaining a shared context.
    \item {SelectorGroupChat}: A language model selects the next speaker after each message, enabling dynamic, model-driven delegation.
    \item {MagenticOneGroupChat}: A generalist multi-agent configuration designed for solving open-ended web and file-based tasks.
    \item {Swarm}: Agents use {HandoffMessage} to explicitly signal transitions to specific other agents, enabling developer-defined handoff patterns.
\end{itemize}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=autogen, caption={AutoGen {RoundRobinGroupChat} with combined termination conditions.}, label={lst:autogen-roundrobin}]

termination = (
    TextMentionTermination("DONE")
    | MaxMessageTermination(max_messages=6)
)

team = RoundRobinGroupChat(
    participants=[researcher, critic],
    termination_condition=termination,
)

\end{lstlisting}

Termination conditions control when a team stops executing. These are passed at team creation and include conditions such as {TextMentionTermination} (stops when a specific word appears), {TextMessageTermination} (stops when a specific agent produces a text message) and {ExternalTermination} (stops from outside the team). Conditions can be combined using bitwise operators \cite{autogen_docs_2025}.


\subsubsection{LangGraph}

LangGraph encodes all coordination logic explicitly through a directed graph composed of nodes and edges. There is no dedicated team, crew, or orchestration abstraction. The graph itself is the coordination mechanism. Nodes represent discrete computational steps (agent calls, tool execution, routing logic) and edges define the transitions between them. This gives developers full control over the execution flow but requires all coordination patterns to be manually constructed.

On a source code level, a graph is built using the {StateGraph} class, which is parameterized with a typed state schema. Nodes are added via {add\_node} and transitions are defined through {add\_edge} for unconditional transitions or {add\_conditional\_edges} for branching based on the output of a node. Conditional edges accept a routing function that inspects the current state and returns the name of the next node to execute.

LangGraph provides prebuilt utilities that implement common coordination patterns. The {create\_react\_agent} function constructs a ReAct-style loop where an agent node and a tool node alternate until the agent produces a final response. For multi-agent coordination, developers manually compose multiple agent nodes within a single graph and define the routing logic between them \cite{langgraph_docs_2025}.


\subsection{Synthesis and Requirements}

The framework analysis of coordination revealed three fundamentally different paradigms. Despite these differences, all three frameworks ultimately express the same fundamental coordination concerns: which agents participate, in what order they act and under what conditions execution branches or terminates. The difference is whether these concerns are declared through configuration (CrewAI's process type), selected from presets (AutoGen's team types), or constructed from primitives (LangGraph's nodes and edges).

From this analysis, the following representational requirements can be derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{R10}] \textbf{Coordination pattern as a named concept.} The ontology must represent coordination patterns (sequential, hierarchical, round-robin, swarm, selector-based) as identifiable concepts, regardless of whether they are selected from a preset (CrewAI, AutoGen) or manually constructed in a graph (LangGraph). This enables cross-framework comparison at the architectural level.

    \item[\textbf{R11}] \textbf{Agent-to-coordination binding.} The ontology must capture which agents participate in a coordination structure and how they are bound to it. Either as members of a crew with assigned tasks (CrewAI), as participants in a team (AutoGen), or as nodes within a graph (LangGraph).

    \item[\textbf{R12}] \textbf{Termination conditions.} All three frameworks define conditions under which execution stops, though the mechanisms differ: CrewAI relies on task completion and flow structure, AutoGen uses explicit termination condition objects and LangGraph terminates when a node routes to the {END} sentinel. The ontology must capture termination semantics independently of these framework-specific mechanisms.

    \item[\textbf{R13}] \textbf{Multi-level coordination.} CrewAI's separation of crews (inner coordination) and flows (outer orchestration) has no direct equivalent in AutoGen or LangGraph, where a single abstraction handles both levels. The ontology must be able to represent nested coordination structures so that a CrewAI flow containing multiple crews can be captured without flattening the hierarchy, while also representing the single-level coordination of the other frameworks.
    
    \item[\textbf{R14}] \textbf{Workflow step structure with typed transitions.} The ontology must represent the execution structure of a coordination pattern as a sequence of discrete steps with typed transitions. This includes distinguishing entry points, exit points, and intermediate steps; representing unconditional and conditional transitions with associated routing logic; and linking each step to its associated task where applicable.

\end{enumerate}


\section{Reasoning Engine}
 
The reasoning engine acts as the cognitive core of the agent, transforming high-level goals into actionable steps through multi-step reasoning and planning \cite{lietal2024}. In contrast to traditional models that provide immediate, single-step responses, agentic AI performs a step-by-step solution process that includes problem analysis, planning, solving, reflection and validation \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}. 
 
To implement this robust cognition, systems employ structured reasoning frameworks such as Chain of Thought (CoT), Tree of Thoughts (ToT) and Graph of Thoughts (GoT) \cite{lietal2024}. These architectural frameworks allow agents to explore multiple partial reasoning chains concurrently, evaluate the viability of intermediate results and perform self-correction before committing to a final action \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}. Furthermore, reasoning processes can be categorized into one-step decision-making, where a task is decomposed and executed in a single logical flow and multi-step reasoning, which requires iterative reasoning cycles that maintain long-term consistency with the overall objective \cite{guo2024largelanguagemodelbased}. 

\subsection{Framework Analysis}

\subsubsection{CrewAI}

CrewAI provides reasoning as an explicit, configurable feature at the agent level. On a source code level, this is achieved by setting a boolean flag {reasoning} in the agent definition. When set to {True}, the agent reflects on a given task, evaluates possible approaches and refines its plan before executing an action. This reflection cycle repeats until the agent converges on a satisfactory plan or a configurable {max\_reasoning\_attempts} threshold, defined as an integer in the agent definition, is reached. Reasoning in CrewAI is therefore a framework-managed capability that is toggled per agent independently of the underlying language model \cite{crewai_docs_2025}.

\subsubsection{AutoGen}

In AutoGen, reasoning is not exposed as a dedicated framework feature but is integrated into its conversation-based architectural paradigm. Whether and how an agent reasons depends primarily on the capabilities of the underlying language model. If a reasoning-capable model such as {o3-mini} is selected as the model client, the model itself handles multi-step reasoning internally without additional framework configuration. Alternatively, reasoning can be achieved architecturally by introducing a dedicated planning agent that decomposes a task into sub-steps using a language model before delegating them to other agents. In this case, reasoning emerges from the orchestration of multiple agents rather than from a single agent's configuration \cite{autogen_docs_2025}.

\subsubsection{LangGraph}

LangGraph does not provide a built-in reasoning mechanism at the agent or node level. Since LangGraph models agentic behavior as explicit graph structures, reasoning patterns must be manually constructed by the developer through the graph topology itself. A Chain of Thought pattern, for instance, can be implemented as a sequence of nodes where each node performs one reasoning step and passes its output via the shared state to the next. More complex patterns such as reflection or self-correction require the developer to define conditional edges that loop back to previous nodes based on evaluation criteria. LangGraph therefore offers maximum flexibility in designing reasoning workflows but places the full architectural decision making on the developer, as no reasoning behavior is provided by default \cite{langgraph_docs_2025}.

\subsection{Synthesis and Requirements}

The framework analysis of reasoning capabilities revealed three different abstraction levels. CrewAI treats reasoning as a framework-managed, declarative feature that is toggled per agent through a boolean flag and bounded by a configurable iteration limit. AutoGen delegates reasoning either to the underlying language model's native capabilities or to the architectural composition of multiple agents, where a dedicated planning agent orchestrates task decomposition. LangGraph provides no built-in reasoning mechanism and instead requires developers to manually encode reasoning patterns through the graph topology itself.

From this analysis, the following representational requirement for reasoning can be derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{R15}] \textbf{Reasoning as capability and pattern.} The ontology must represent reasoning on three dimensions: (1) whether an agent has reasoning enabled as a declarative capability, (2) which reasoning pattern is employed if identifiable, and (3) whether reasoning originates from the framework, the language model's native capabilities, or the system's graph topology.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Memory Architecture}

Memory provides agents with temporal continuity and the foundational knowledge required to navigate complex environments \cite{fi17090404}. The architecture is typically divided into distinct functional durations. Short-term memory (or working memory) maintains the immediate conversational or task context within the language model's context window constraints \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures} \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}. Long-term memory, conversely, captures persistent data across sessions, allowing the agent to recall historical interactions, user preferences and learned knowledge over time \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures}.
 
Beyond duration, long-term memory can be functionally categorized into semantic memory for storing facts and reasoning paths, procedural memory for recalling specific task flows and strategies and episodic memory for retaining detailed contextual snapshots of specific past events \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures}. To manage and access this amount of information, agents frequently utilize Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and external vector databases. These systems retrieve relevant text snippets based on semantic similarity and append them to the agent's prompts, thereby reducing hallucinations and supporting dynamic learning \cite{guo2024largelanguagemodelbased} \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}. Memory operations also involve active reflection, where agents summarize and distill past experiences to continually enhance their cognitive abilities and adaptability \cite{lietal2024}. 

\subsection{Framework Analysis}

\subsubsection{CrewAI}

CrewAI provides an integrated {Memory} class that supports short-term, long-term, entity and external memory types. This class uses a language model to analyze content at storage time, inferring scope, categories and importance. The memory system can be used as a standalone component, at the agent level, at the crew level and within flows.

At the crew level, all agents in a crew share the crew's memory unless an individual agent has its own. After each task, the crew automatically extracts facts from the task output and stores them. Before the next task, relevant context is recalled from memory and injected into the task prompt at runtime. At the agent level, agents can maintain private memory that is not shared with the rest of the crew. Standalone memory acts as a general-purpose knowledge base that is independent of any specific agent or crew \cite{crewai_docs_2025}.


\subsubsection{AutoGen}

AutoGen provides a {Memory} protocol that defines a standard interface for storing and retrieving contextual information. The protocol specifies methods for adding entries, querying relevant content, updating an agent's model context and clearing the store. The simplest implementation is {ListMemory}, which maintains memory entries in chronological order and appends them to the model's context before each task execution.

On a source code level, memory is assigned to an agent by passing a list of memory instances during agent instantiation via the {memory} parameter. At runtime, the agent queries its memory stores before processing a task and the retrieved content is injected into the model context as a system message. Memory in AutoGen is therefore agent-scoped: each agent manages its own memory instances independently.

For more advanced use cases, AutoGen provides memory extensions such as ChromaDBVectorMemory and {RedisMemory}, which implement the same {Memory} protocol but use vector similarity search for retrieval rather than chronological ordering. Since all implementations conform to the same protocol, they are interchangeable at the source code level without modifying the agent's logic \cite{autogen_docs_2025}.

\subsubsection{LangGraph}

LangGraph implements memory through two distinct mechanisms that map to different persistence scopes. Short-term memory is provided through \textit{checkpointers}, which persist the graph's state across invocations within the same thread. On a source code level, a checkpointer is instantiated and passed to the graph at compile time via the {checkpointer} parameter. Each invocation is associated with a {thread\_id} and the checkpointer automatically saves and restores the full graph state between turns, enabling multi-turn conversations.

Long-term memory is implemented through a separate \textit{store} abstraction, which persists data across threads and sessions. A store is similarly passed to the graph at compile time via the {store} parameter. Unlike the checkpointer, which operates transparently on the entire graph state, the store must be explicitly accessed within node functions to read and write memory entries. Entries are organized into namespaces, typically scoped by user or application context \cite{langgraph_docs_2025}.


\subsection{Synthesis and Requirements}

CrewAI provides a unified memory system that operates at both crew and agent level, with automatic fact extraction and injection at runtime. AutoGen exposes memory through a protocol-based interface that is strictly agent-scoped, with no built-in shared memory across agents. LangGraph separates memory into two distinct mechanisms (i.e. checkpointers for short-term state persistence and stores for long-term data) both scoped to the graph as a whole and accessed explicitly within node functions.

From this analysis, the following representational requirements can be derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{R16}] \textbf{Memory scope as an explicit property.} The ontology must represent at which level memory is owned and accessible: agent-private, shared among a group, or global to the system.

    \item[\textbf{R17}] \textbf{Separation of short-term and long-term persistence.} The ontology must represent the lifecycle of memory, whether it persists within a single execution, across executions within a thread, or across all sessions independently of how a given framework packages its memory features.

\end{enumerate}


\section{Tool Integration}

Tool integration significantly expands an agent's capabilities beyond its static, pre-trained knowledge by enabling it to interact directly with external systems and physical or digital environments \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}. By dynamically invoking functionalities such as web browsers, calculators, code interpreters and specialized APIs, agents can retrieve real-time data, perform complex computations and execute software commands \cite{lietal2024} \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}. The process of utilizing a tool involves several seamless sub-tasks: intent recognition to determine when a tool is needed, function selection to choose the most appropriate tool, parameter-value mapping to extract required arguments and the actual execution and response generation \cite{yehudai2025surveyevaluationllmbasedagents}.

Advanced agentic systems are not just limited to using pre-existing tools; they also demonstrate the ability to autonomously select, combine and even create new tools. Because language models can generate executable code, agents can write new Python utility functions or scripts on the fly, verify them through unit tests and deploy them as custom tools to solve novel problems. This dynamic tool integration reduces computational errors, enhances the interpretability of the agent's actions and embeds operational rules in a clear, reliable manner \cite{schneider2025generativeagenticaisurvey}.

\subsection{Framework Analysis}

\subsubsection{CrewAI}
CrewAI defines a tool as a skill or function that agents can utilise to perform actions. Tools can be assigned at two levels: at the agent level, where they become available across all tasks the agent executes and at the task level, where they override the agent's default tool set for that specific task.

On a source code level, custom tools are created either by subclassing {BaseTool} or by using the {@tool} decorator on a function. When subclassing, the developer defines a {name}, a {description}, an input schema via {args\_schema} (a Pydantic model) and implements the {\_run} method containing the tool's logic. When using the decorator, the function's docstring serves as the tool description that the agent uses to decide when to invoke it. CrewAI also provides a large library of pre-built tools (e.g. {SerperDevTool}, {FileReadTool}, {PDFSearchTool}) that can be instantiated and assigned to agents or tasks without custom implementation \cite{crewai_docs_2025}.


\subsubsection{AutoGen}

AutoGen enables tool use through the {AssistantAgent}, which accepts tools via the {tools} parameter. A tool is most commonly defined as a Python function, which the agent automatically converts into a {FunctionTool} at runtime. The tool schema — including name, description and parameter types — is automatically generated from the function's signature and docstring and provided to the language model during execution.

Beyond individual tools, AutoGen introduces the concept of a \textit{Workbench}, which is a collection of tools that share state and resources. The primary example is {McpWorkbench}, which connects to a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server and exposes its tools to the agent. A workbench is passed to the agent via the {workbench} parameter and operates alongside any individually assigned tools \cite{autogen_docs_2025}.



\subsubsection{LangGraph}
LangGraph implements tool use through LangChain's tool infrastructure. Tools are defined as Python functions using the {@tool} decorator, where the function's docstring serves as the description and type hints define the input schema. For more complex inputs, a Pydantic model or JSON schema can be specified via the {args\_schema} parameter.

Tools are integrated into the graph through a prebuilt {ToolNode}, which handles tool execution, parallel tool calls and error handling. The {ToolNode} is added to the graph like any other node and connected to the model node via a conditional edge ({tools\_condition}) that routes execution to the tool node only when the model generates a tool call \cite{langgraph_docs_2025}.

\subsection{Synthesis and Requirements}
All three frameworks share the fundamental concept of a tool as a callable function with a name, description and typed input schema that is exposed to a language model. The definition mechanism is also convergent: all frameworks support defining tools as decorated Python functions with auto-generated schemas. The key differences lie in how tools are assigned, scoped and integrated into the execution flow.

From this analysis, the following representational requirements can be derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{R18}] \textbf{Tool as a framework-independent concept.} The ontology must represent tools as entities with a name, description and input schema, independent of the framework-specific definition mechanism (decorator, subclass, or function).

    \item[\textbf{R19}] \textbf{Tool assignment scope.} The ontology must capture at which level a tool is bound: to an individual agent (all frameworks), to a specific task (CrewAI), or to a graph node (LangGraph). 
\end{enumerate}

\section{Guardrails and Governance Mechanisms}

Because agentic AI operates with high autonomy in dynamic environments, robust guardrails are essential to ensure that systems act safely, predictably and in alignment with human values. Guardrails consist of embedded technical constraints. Including goal safety protocols, schema validators and fail-safe mechanisms—that monitor and validate agent outputs before execution, preventing harmful actions or deviations from assigned objectives. By incorporating node validation, retry logic and early-stage trust layers, developers can secure agent workflows and mitigate risks like malicious code execution or data privacy breaches  \cite{deepaketal2025} \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures}.

On a broader systemic level, governance frameworks dictate the degree of human oversight and accountability required for agentic operations. This is frequently implemented through Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) architectures, where a human must explicitly approve critical decisions, or Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL) designs, where humans monitor autonomous execution with the power to intervene via override protocols. Governance also heavily relies on Explainable AI (XAI) techniques and comprehensive audit trails to log reasoning steps and tool invocations, ensuring that the agent's decision-making process remains transparent, legally compliant and accountable when errors occur \cite{deepaketal2025}. 

\subsection{Framework Analysis}

\subsubsection{CrewAI}
CrewAI implements guardrails at the task level through the {guardrail} and {guardrails} parameters. Two types are supported: function-based guardrails, which are Python functions that receive a {TaskOutput} and return a tuple indicating success or failure with feedback and LLM-based guardrails, which are string descriptions that the agent's language model evaluates against the output. Multiple guardrails can be chained sequentially, with each receiving the output of the previous one. A configurable {guardrail\_max\_retries} parameter limits how many times an agent reattempts a task after guardrail failure \cite{crewai_docs_2025}.

\subsubsection{AutoGen}
AutoGen does not provide a dedicated guardrail mechanism at the task or agent level. Output validation must be implemented by the developer, typically through a dedicated critic agent that evaluates outputs and provides feedback within the team's conversational flow. This is a common architectural pattern in AutoGen rather than a framework feature: a validator agent is included as a team participant and a {TextMentionTermination} condition (e.g. listening for ``APPROVE'') controls when execution stops.

Human-in-the-loop is supported through two complementary mechanisms. First, a {UserProxyAgent} can be included as a participant in a team, prompting the user for input during its turn via a configurable input function. This approach is synchronous and blocks the team's execution until the user responds. Second, the team can be configured to pause after a set number of agent turns using the {max\_turns} parameter, or to stop when an agent issues a {HandoffMessage} targeting the user, detected by a {HandoffTermination} condition. In both cases, the team preserves its state after stopping and can be resumed with user feedback passed as the {task} parameter in the next call to {run} \cite{autogen_docs_2025}.


\subsubsection{LangGraph}
LangGraph does not provide built-in guardrails but offers comprehensive mechanisms for human-in-the-loop through its {interrupt} function. Unlike static breakpoints (which pause before or after specific nodes at compile time), interrupts are dynamic: they can be placed anywhere within a node's logic and can be conditional based on application state. When {interrupt} is called, graph execution is suspended, the current state is saved via the checkpointer and a payload is surfaced to the caller. Execution resumes when the caller re-invokes the graph with a {Command(resume=...)} containing the human's response, which becomes the return value of the {interrupt} call inside the node.

This mechanism supports several human-in-the-loop patterns at the source code level: approval workflows where a node pauses for a binary approve/reject decision, review-and-edit patterns where a human can modify LLM outputs before they are committed to state and input validation loops where an interrupt repeatedly prompts the user until valid input is received. Interrupts can also be placed inside tool functions, causing the tool to pause for approval whenever it is invoked.

Guardrail-like validation must be implemented as a dedicated node in the graph that inspects the state and routes execution to either the next step or back to the producing node via conditional edges \cite{langgraph_docs_2025}.

\subsection{Synthesis and Requirements}
The frameworks diverge significantly in how they handle validation and human oversight. CrewAI provides guardrails as a first-class, configurable feature at the task level with built-in retry logic and supports human-in-the-loop through task-level flags and flow-level decorators. AutoGen has no dedicated guardrail mechanism; validation is achieved through critic agents participating in the conversational flow, while human-in-the-loop is supported through {UserProxyAgent} participation, {max\_turns} pausing and {HandoffTermination} conditions. LangGraph requires guardrail logic to be encoded as validation nodes with conditional edges and provides a dynamic {interrupt} function that can be placed anywhere within node logic for human review, approval, or input validation.

From this analysis, the following representational requirements can be derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{R20}] \textbf{Guardrails as a task-level property.} The ontology must represent that a task or step has associated validation logic, regardless of whether it is implemented as a task parameter (CrewAI), a validator agent (AutoGen), or a conditional node (LangGraph). The representation captures that validation exists and what criteria it checks, without modeling the implementation mechanism itself.

    \item[\textbf{R21}] \textbf{Human-in-the-loop as an explicit checkpoint.} The ontology must represent points in the workflow where human review or input is required. This includes CrewAI's {human\_input} flag and {@human\_feedback} decorator, AutoGen's {UserProxyAgent} and {HandoffTermination} targeting the user and LangGraph's {interrupt} calls within nodes. The semantic representation captures the presence and position of human checkpoints so that the transformation method can reconstruct an equivalent mechanism in the target framework.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{A Framework-Agnostic Semantic Representation}
\label{chap:ontology}
The previous chapter analyzed how agentic AI concepts are realized across three frameworks and derived a set of representational requirements from those findings. These requirements describe what a framework-independent 
semantic representation must be able to express in order to support interoperability. This chapter presents the ontology developed to meet those requirements. The ontology is the first of the two artefacts of this thesis. Its purpose is specific: it serves as the common intermediate representation and needs to be expressive enough for framework-agnostic source code extraction and generation. 

The ontology is developed following the Ontology Development 101 methodology proposed by \cite{onto101}. The first step of this methodology is to determine the domain and scope of the ontology, which is defined by requirement elicitation \ref{chap:requirements}. 

\section{Scope and Competency Questions}

 The scope is defined through competency questions, as introduced by \cite{gruningerandfox}. Competency questions are natural language queries that a fully populated ontology must be able to answer. They act as boundary conditions: if a populated ontology instance cannot answer a competency question, either the ontology is missing a required concept or the extraction has not captured it  \cite{onto101}. In this thesis, the competency questions are derived directly from the requirements established in Chapter~\ref{chap:requirements}. The corresponding competency question can be found in \ref{tab:competency-questions} and are evaluated in the ontology evaluation \ref{sec:roundtrip-evaluation}.


\section{Reuse Evaluation}

The ontology is not built from scratch. AgentO \cite{ekelhart2026agento} provides the conceptual foundation, defining core classes such as \texttt{LLMAgent}, \texttt{Task}, \texttt{Tool}, \texttt{Prompt} and \texttt{WorkflowStep} along with 22 object properties and 10 datatype properties. Where AgentO's existing vocabulary satisfies a requirement, it is reused without modification. Where it falls short, new classes and properties are introduced. All extensions are strictly additive: no existing AgentO class is removed or structurally modified and no existing property has its domain or range altered. Other concepts and properties derived from literature review in \ref{chap:requirements}, while not defined as ontology classes and properties, were also considered in reuse evaluation (e.g. the unified data class model proposed by \cite{derouiche2025agenticaiframeworksarchitectures})

\subsection{Agent Definition}

\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-agent}

AgentO already provides the \texttt{LLMAgent} and \texttt{Prompt} classes,
which together cover R1 (framework-independent agent identity) and R4
(flexible attribute cardinality) without modification. AgentOSCIN extends
this baseline with properties that carry the remaining agent-level
requirements.

The datatype property \texttt{agentType} (R5) types each agent by its functional
role in the coordination structure rather than by its implementation
class. The decision to model this as a property rather than as a subclass
hierarchy reflects the cross-framework variability of the concept: a
manager agent in CrewAI is structurally different from a
\texttt{UserProxyAgent} in AutoGen, and both differ from a routing node
in LangGraph~\cite{crewai_docs_2025,autogen_docs_2025,langgraph_concepts}. A property-based encoding allows
the same \texttt{LLMAgent} individual to be classified consistently
regardless of how its host framework expresses the role.

R2 (orchestration- versus model-relevant directives) and R3 (prompt
decomposition) are handled at the \texttt{Prompt} level rather than at
the agent level. Distinguishing the directive function of a prompt
fragment is an attribute of the prompt itself, not of the agent that
carries it, and decomposing a prompt into structured components only
makes sense once the prompt is treated as a first-class entity.
AgentO's existing \texttt{Prompt} class is therefore retained as the
basis for two new datatype properties: \texttt{hasDirectiveFunction},
which records the function a prompt fragment serves (e.g.\ role
instruction, context, backstory), and \texttt{hasSourceAttribute},
which preserves the name of the source-code attribute the prompt was
extracted from (e.g.\ \texttt{goal}, \texttt{backstory},
\texttt{system\_message}). This pair allows both the semantic role and
the framework-specific origin of a prompt to survive the round trip
through the ontology.

\subsection{Perception and Task Definition}
\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-task}

The \texttt{Task} class is inherited from AgentO and satisfies R6
directly. AgentOSCIN adds several properties that capture task-level
information not represented in AgentO.

\texttt{performedByAgent} (R7) records the agent responsible for a task.
The associated datatype property \texttt{hasDelegationStrategy} encodes how that
assignment was determined: explicit assignment in the source
configuration, structural assignment through graph topology, or
delegation deferred to runtime. Recording the strategy alongside the
assignment preserves a distinction that is otherwise lost when the
ontology is queried, since a CrewAI hierarchical process and a CrewAI
sequential process produce structurally similar
\texttt{performedByAgent} assertions for different reasons~\cite{crewai_docs_2025}.

The object property \texttt{dependsOn} (R8) links one \texttt{Task} to another
\texttt{Task} it depends on. The accompanying datatype property
\texttt{hasDependencyType} normalizes the three frameworks' distinct
dependency mechanisms into a unified vocabulary. CrewAI's
\texttt{context} attribute, LangGraph's edges, and AutoGen's implicit
sequential ordering are heterogeneous on the surface but encode the same
underlying relation between tasks~\cite{crewai_docs_2025,langgraph_concepts,autogen_docs_2025}. The
\texttt{hasDependencyType} value distinguishes these origins so that the
generation direction can produce idiomatic code in each target framework.

\texttt{hasExpectedOutput} and \texttt{hasOutputSchema} (R9) separate
the natural-language description of an expected output from its
structured schema. The separation reflects the observation that all
three frameworks support the former while only CrewAI consistently
supports the latter, and that conflating them would force the ontology
to either lose information or produce unreliable schemas during
generation. The object property \texttt{hasOutputSchema} links a
\texttt{Task} (or \texttt{Orchestration}) to a \texttt{Schema} individual,
while \texttt{hasExpectedOutput} is a datatype property carrying the
natural-language specification as a string.

\texttt{Schema} is a new class introduced to represent structured-output
schemas (e.g.\ JSON~Schema, Pydantic models). Its datatype property
\texttt{hasSchemaDefinition} stores the serialized schema content. Modeling
the schema as a separate class rather than a string property on the task
allows the same schema to be referenced by multiple tasks or by an
orchestration, and supports future extensions such as schema validation
or compatibility checking.


\subsection{Coordination}
\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-coordination}

The coordination concept group is the most extensively modified part of
the ontology, reflecting the fact that AgentO does not represent
coordination patterns or termination semantics as first-class concepts.
Five new classes and several supporting properties are introduced.

\subsubsection{System Structure}

The triple \texttt{AgenticSystem}, \texttt{Orchestration}, \texttt{Team}
(R13) provides the multi-level coordination structure required to
represent CrewAI flows containing multiple crews without flattening the
hierarchy. \texttt{AgenticSystem} is the top-level container and subclass
of \texttt{beam:System}; it is connected to its parts through
\texttt{containsAgent}, \texttt{containsTeam} and
\texttt{containsOrchestration}. The datatype property
\texttt{hasSourceFramework} records which framework the system was
extracted from, enabling framework-aware queries and generation.

\texttt{Orchestration} is modeled as a subclass of \texttt{Team},
representing the outer flow that orchestrates one or more inner teams
via \texttt{orchestratesTeam}. A flat AutoGen team or a single
LangGraph graph is represented as an \texttt{AgenticSystem} containing
exactly one \texttt{Team} and no \texttt{Orchestration}, ensuring that
the same ontological structure accommodates both nested and single-level
cases.

\subsubsection{Coordination Patterns}

\texttt{CoordinationPattern} (R10) models the coordination strategy as
a named concept rather than encoding it as a string-valued property on
\texttt{Team}. A property-valued
encoding would prevent reasoning over the pattern itself, for instance
asking whether two teams use compatible coordination strategies or
whether a given pattern is supported by a target framework. With
\texttt{CoordinationPattern} as a class, individual patterns
can be defined as named individuals and queried directly.

The ontology defines eight \texttt{CoordinationPattern} individuals:
\texttt{Sequential}, \texttt{Hierarchical}, \texttt{RoundRobin},
\texttt{SelectorBased}, \texttt{Swarm}, \texttt{ReActLoop},
\texttt{Network} and \texttt{Custom}. These cover the coordination
strategies observed across all three frameworks: \texttt{Sequential}
and \texttt{Hierarchical} map directly to CrewAI's process types,
\texttt{RoundRobin}, \texttt{SelectorBased} and \texttt{Swarm} capture
AutoGen's team types, \texttt{ReActLoop} represents the
tool-calling agent pattern used in LangGraph's
\texttt{create\_react\_agent}, \texttt{Network} covers general
graph-based coordination, and \texttt{Custom} serves as a fallback for
patterns not matching any predefined category. The object property
\texttt{employsCoordinationPattern} links a \texttt{Team} to its
pattern.

\subsubsection{Workflow Structure}

\texttt{WorkflowPattern} and \texttt{WorkflowStep} (R14) are inherited
from AgentO and decompose the internal execution structure of a
coordination unit. AgentOSCIN introduces \texttt{ConditionalStep} as a
subclass of \texttt{WorkflowStep}, attaching
the datatype property \texttt{hasRoutingLogic} at this level. This avoids polluting unconditional
steps with a property they cannot use and lets the generator emit
unconditional or conditional transitions based on the step's class
rather than on a runtime check.

Three additional datatype properties on \texttt{WorkflowStep} preserve
framework-specific decorator and wiring information needed for faithful
code regeneration:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{hasDecoratorArgument} stores the original literal
argument(s) of the framework decorator that defined the step (e.g.\
CrewAI's \texttt{@start("wait\_next\_run")} or
\texttt{@listen("label")}). These arguments are not method names and
therefore cannot be reconstructed from inverse \texttt{nextStep} edges
alone.

\item \texttt{hasAggregationCombinator} records the combinator used to
aggregate multiple predecessor steps in the source decorator (e.g.\
CrewAI's \texttt{@listen(or\_(step\_a, step\_b))} stores
\texttt{"or\_"}). Values are \texttt{"or\_"}, \texttt{"and\_"}, or empty
for single-predecessor steps.

\item \texttt{callsCrew} stores the key of the team or crew invoked
by a workflow step (e.g.\ a CrewAI \texttt{@listen} method calling
\texttt{crew.kickoff()}), enabling per-step crew binding through the
ontology round trip.
\end{itemize}

The object property \texttt{hasAssociatedAgent} links a
\texttt{WorkflowStep} to the \texttt{LLMAgent} that executes it,
capturing the pattern used in LangGraph where a node function is
implemented by a specific agent.

\subsubsection{Termination Conditions}

\texttt{TerminationCondition} (R12) follows the same class-based
modeling principle as \texttt{CoordinationPattern}.
AutoGen's termination conditions are first-class objects in the source
language and combine using bitwise operators~\cite{autogen_docs_2025}; representing
them as ontology individuals preserves this compositional structure.
The object property \texttt{hasTerminationCondition} links a
\texttt{Team} to its termination condition.

Five disjoint subclasses cover the observed termination strategies:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{TurnLimitTermination} — execution stops after a
maximum number of turns, recorded in \texttt{hasMaxTurns}
(\texttt{xsd:integer}).

\item \texttt{TaskCompletionTermination} — execution stops when all
assigned tasks have completed.

\item \texttt{EventBasedTermination} — execution stops when a
specific event or condition occurs, described by
\texttt{hasTriggerExpression} (\texttt{xsd:string}).

\item \texttt{RoutingTermination} — execution stops when a routing
decision leads to the end of the workflow.

\item \texttt{CompositeTermination} — combines multiple termination
conditions via a logical operator. The datatype property
\texttt{hasOperator} records the combinator (e.g.\ \texttt{"AND"},
\texttt{"OR"}) and the object property \texttt{hasSubCondition}
links to the constituent \texttt{TerminationCondition} individuals.
\end{itemize}

An \texttt{AllDisjointClasses} axiom enforces mutual exclusivity
among these five subclasses.

R11 (agent-to-coordination binding) is satisfied by the existing
\texttt{containsAgent} relation from \texttt{AgenticSystem} together
with the \texttt{performedByAgent} relation on tasks. No new property
is required.

\subsection{Reasoning}
\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-reasoning}

R15 requires three distinct pieces of information to be representable:
whether reasoning is enabled, what pattern it follows, and where the
capability originates. AgentOSCIN models this with one new class and
several properties on \texttt{LLMAgent}.

\texttt{ReasoningPattern} is the new class, linked to agents via the
object property \texttt{employsReasoningPattern}. Five subclasses
enumerate the patterns observed across the analyzed frameworks:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{ChainOfThought} — step-by-step sequential reasoning.
\item \texttt{ReAct} — interleaved reasoning and action.
\item \texttt{ReflectionLoop} — iterative self-critique and refinement.
\item \texttt{TreeOfThoughts} — branching exploration of reasoning paths.
\item \texttt{Unspecified} — reasoning is enabled but no explicit pattern is identifiable from the source code.
\end{itemize}

The datatype property
\texttt{hasReasoningEnabled} (\texttt{xsd:boolean}) records whether reasoning is active,
\texttt{hasReasoningOrigin} (\texttt{xsd:string}) records whether the capability comes from
the framework, the underlying language model, or the system topology,
and \texttt{hasMaxReasoningAttempts} (\texttt{xsd:integer}) captures an
optional upper bound on reasoning iterations.

Modeling reasoning origin as a property rather than encoding it
implicitly in the agent's class is a deliberate choice driven by the
extraction direction. CrewAI exposes reasoning as a configurable agent
flag~\cite{crewai_docs_2025}, AutoGen leaves it to the underlying
model~\cite{autogen_docs_2025}, and LangGraph requires it to be constructed in the
graph topology~\cite{langgraph_concepts}.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-memory}

The memory concept group introduces the \texttt{MemoryBinding} class to
satisfy R16 (memory scope) and R17 (persistence separation). The
binding is a reified relation between a memory owner (an
\texttt{LLMAgent}, \texttt{Team}, or \texttt{AgenticSystem}) and a
\texttt{Memory} individual rather than a direct property. Reification
is required because the same memory store can be bound at different
scopes in different parts of a system. A CrewAI crew-level memory and a
CrewAI agent-level memory may reference the same underlying
store~\cite{crewai_docs_2025} but differ in their access scope, and a direct
property between agent and memory cannot distinguish these cases.

The object property \texttt{bindsMemory} connects a
\texttt{MemoryBinding} to the \texttt{Memory} it wraps, while
\texttt{memoryBoundTo} connects the binding to its owner (an
\texttt{LLMAgent}, \texttt{Team}, or \texttt{AgenticSystem}).
Two specialized sub-properties of the generic \texttt{hasMemoryBinding}
refine the attachment point: \texttt{hasAgentMemoryBinding} restricts
the domain to \texttt{LLMAgent} and \texttt{hasTeamMemoryBinding}
restricts the domain to \texttt{Team}.

\texttt{hasMemoryScope} (\texttt{xsd:string}) on \texttt{MemoryBinding} records whether the
binding is agent-private, group-shared, or system-global.
\texttt{hasPersistenceScope} (\texttt{xsd:string}) on \texttt{Memory} records whether the memory persists within
a single execution, across executions on a thread, or across all
sessions. Separating these dimensions preserves the lifecycle
distinctions captured in LangGraph's checkpointer-versus-store
split~\cite{langgraph_concepts} while allowing the same dimensional vocabulary to
describe CrewAI's and AutoGen's memory configurations.

\subsection{Tools}
\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-tools}

R18 (framework-independent tool concept) is largely covered by
AgentO's existing \texttt{Tool} class. AgentOSCIN adds two datatype
properties. \texttt{hasInputSchema} captures the typed input
specification that all three frameworks generate from Python type hints,
Pydantic models, or JSON schemas. \texttt{hasImplementationReference}
records a pointer to the tool's implementation (e.g.\ a module path or
external package identifier), preserving provenance information needed
for code generation when a tool is imported rather than defined inline.

R19 (tool assignment scope) is satisfied by distinguishing two object
properties: \texttt{agentToolUsage} (inherited from AgentO) and
\texttt{taskToolUsage} (introduced by AgentOSCIN as a sub-property of
\texttt{toolUsage}).
Modeling the assignment as two separate properties rather than as a
single property with a scope qualifier reflects the semantics of the
target frameworks: agent-level and task-level tool assignment are not
the same relation and conflating them would force the generator to
infer scope at code emission time.

\subsection{Guardrails and Governance}
\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-guardrails}

The governance concept group introduces two new classes to satisfy R20
and R21, both of which describe concepts entirely absent from AgentO.

\texttt{Guardrail} (R20) attaches to \texttt{Task} via the
\texttt{hasGuardrail} object property and carries three datatype
properties: \texttt{hasGuardrailType}
to distinguish function-based, LLM-based, and structural validation
mechanisms; \texttt{hasValidationLogic} to store the validation
expression or function reference; and \texttt{hasMaxRetries}
(\texttt{xsd:integer}) to record the maximum number of retry attempts
when validation fails. The class-level modeling is necessary because a
task may have multiple guardrails chained
sequentially~\cite{crewai_docs_2025}, which a flat property could not
represent.

\texttt{HumanCheckpoint} (R21) attaches to \texttt{Task} via
\texttt{hasHumanCheckpoint} and carries three datatype properties:
\texttt{hasCheckpointType} to
distinguish the three observed checkpoint mechanisms (approval,
review-and-edit, and input-validation);
\texttt{hasCheckpointPosition} to record where the checkpoint sits in
the task execution flow; and \texttt{isMandatory}
(\texttt{xsd:boolean}) to indicate whether the checkpoint can be
bypassed. Modeling human-in-the-loop as a
positional checkpoint rather than as a boolean flag captures the
information needed by R21, namely the position of the checkpoint in
the workflow rather than merely its existence.

The auxiliary class \texttt{HumanAgent} (defined in AgentO) extends the
agent hierarchy to represent users participating in agentic systems
through proxies such as AutoGen's
\texttt{UserProxyAgent}~\cite{autogen_docs_2025}. The
\texttt{humanParticipatedIn} relation links a \texttt{HumanAgent} to
the tasks or checkpoints it interacts with, enabling the ontology to
distinguish agent-driven and human-driven contributions to a workflow
without reclassifying users as ordinary agents.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:AgentOSCIN-summary}

AgentOSCIN extends AgentO with nine new classes and a corresponding set 
of object and data properties, all introduced as additive extensions 
that leave the AgentO baseline structurally intact. Every extension 
traces back to a requirement in Chapter~\ref{chap:requirements} and is 
positioned in Figure~\ref{fig:AgentOSCIN} alongside its 
requirement tag. The next chapter shows how the resulting vocabulary 
is exercised by the bidirectional transformation method, both as a 
target for extraction from framework-specific source code and as a 
source for code regeneration.

\chapter{Bidirectional Transformation Method}
\label{chap:transformation}

This chapter presents the transformation method that links framework-specific source code to the semantic representation developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:ontology}. The method is grounded in the OSCIN
framework~\cite{oscin} for ontology-based source code interoperability, with two extensions motivated by the specific characteristics of the agentic AI domain. Table~\ref{tab:oscin-mapping} maps the OSCIN phases to this thesis context and states the scoping decisions. Section~\ref{sec:extraction2} presents the extraction direction: three framework-specific extractors that transform Python source code into ontology instances. Section~\ref{sec:generation} presents the generation direction, which is based on a a template-based code synthesiser that transforms ontology instances into executable source code.

\subsection{Extraction Method}

In this phase, framework-specific source code patterns are mapped to a unified semantic representation. These mappings serve as the foundation for the automated transformation into the domain ontology \cite{oscin}. 

The extraction process is structured into three distinct layers to ensure framework-agnostic extensibility:

\begin{enumerate} \item \textbf{Framework-Specific Parsing (Layer 1):} Individual parsers use Python's \texttt{ast} (Abstract Syntax Tree) module to statically analyze source files.  

\item \textbf{Shared Intermediate Representation (Layer 2):} The output of Layer 1 is mapped to a set of framework-neutral dataclasses (e.g., \texttt{ExtractedAgent}, \texttt{ExtractedTask}, \texttt{ExtractedFlow}). This Shared Intermediate Representation (SIR) acts as a structural contract, abstracting away framework-specific terminology. 

\item \textbf{Ontology Population (Layer 3):} A framework-agnostic populator takes the SIR objects as input to instantiate individuals within the \textit{AgentOSCIN} ontology using \texttt{rdflib} \cite{rdflib_docs}. 
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Framework-Specific Parsing via Static Analysis (Layer 1)}
\label{sec:extraction}

The first layer employs static program analysis to interpret the syntactic topology of the framework-specific source-code. By utilizing Python's built-in ast module, the parser constructs an Abstract Syntax Tree of the target source files \cite{python_ast_docs}. The parsers implement the Visitor pattern (via ast.walk or ast.NodeVisitor) to traverse the tree and identify framework-specific syntactic structures. For instance, the AutoGenParser scans for variable assignments where the value is an instantiation of AssistantAgent, extracting the arguments passed to the constructor. Conversely, the CrewAIParser detects classes decorated with @CrewBase and extracts functions decorated with @agent or @task, simultaneously resolving external YAML configuration files bound to the class attributes.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Simplified AST traversal for AutoGen agent extraction.}, label={lst:ast_parse}] for node in ast.walk(tree): # Detect assignment operations (e.g., researcher = AssistantAgent(...)) if isinstance(node, ast.Assign) and isinstance(node.value, ast.Call): if getattr(node.value.func, "id", "") == "AssistantAgent": agent_name = extract_kwarg(node.value, "name") sys_msg = extract_kwarg(node.value, "system_message")

        # Map to Shared Intermediate Representation
        extracted_agents.append(ExtractedAgent(
            role=agent_name,
            backstory=backstory
        ))
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Shared Intermediate Representation (Layer 2)}

To decouple the extraction logic from the ontology population logic, Layer 1 maps all framework-specific abstractions into a SIR. The SIR is implemented as a set of strictly typed Python dataclasses. This layer acts as an ontological proxy. This proxy normalizes framework implementations into a unified schema. For example, both an AutoGen AssistantAgent and a LangGraph add\_node() invocation are reduced to an ExtractedAgent object, capturing only the semantic essence required by the ontology (e.g., role, tools, language model).

\subsubsection{Ontology Population and Semantic Assertion (Layer 3)}
\label{sec:extraction2}

The final layer iterates over the SIR objects to populate the \textit{AgentOSCIN} ontology using the rdflib library. This process involves minting unique Uniform Resource Identifiers (URIs) for each extracted construct and asserting Resource Description Framework (RDF) triples.

The populator enforces the semantic rules defined in the TBox (schema). For example, when instantiating an agent, the populator creates an individual of type AgentOSCIN:LLMAgent, generates an associated AgentOSCIN:Prompt individual to store the backstory, and asserts an AgentOSCIN:useLanguageModel object property linking the agent to its designated LLM.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Ontology population mapping SIR objects to OWL individuals using RDFLib.}, label={lst:rdflib_pop}] for agent in sir.agents: agent_uri = EX[f"Agent_{sanitize(agent.name)}"]

# Assert Class Type
graph.add((agent_uri, RDF.type, AgentOSCIN.LLMAgent))
graph.add((agent_uri, AgentOSCIN.agentRole, Literal(agent.role)))

# Assert Prompt Object Property
if agent.backstory:
    prompt_uri = EX[f"AgentPrompt_{sanitize(agent.name)}"]
    graph.add((prompt_uri, RDF.type, AgentOSCIN.Prompt))
    graph.add((prompt_uri, AgentOSCIN.promptContext, Literal(agent.backstory)))
    graph.add((agent_uri, AgentOSCIN.agentPrompt, prompt_uri))
\end{lstlisting}


\subsection{Source Code Generation and Interoperability}
\label{sec:generation}

In this thesis, we extend the scope of this final phase. Rather than strictly querying the ontology for analytical purposes as done by \cite{oscin}, we utilize the semantic graph as a middleman to enable bidirectional interoperability as our application case. This is achieved through the reverse transformation and by translating the populated, semantically enriched \textit{AgentOSCIN} ontology back into executable source code for Agentic AI frameworks.

The generation pipeline operates in a reversed three-layer process:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ontology Reading (Layer 1):} A framework-agnostic reader utilizes \texttt{rdflib} to parse the serialized OWL ontology (e.g., Turtle format). It traverses the semantic ABox graph, identifying instances of core conceptual classes (e.g., \texttt{AgentOSCIN:LLMAgent}, \texttt{AgentOSCIN:WorkflowStep}) and resolving their associated object properties. This effectively acts as an advanced realization of OSCIN's Application Perspective Phase.
    
    \item \textbf{Shared Intermediate Representation (Layer 2):} The queried semantic data is reconstructed into the identical Shared Intermediate Representation (SIR) dataclasses used during extraction. 
    
    \item \textbf{Framework-Specific Generation (Layer 3):} Dedicated generator modules (e.g., \texttt{AutoGenGenerator}, \texttt{LangGraphGenerator}) consume the populated SIR objects. Using templating engines (e.g., Jinja2) and abstract syntax manipulation, this layer synthesizes the syntactically correct boilerplate, configuration files, and orchestrational logic required by the target framework.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Ontology Reading and IR Reconstruction (Layers 1 \& 2)}

The reader component serves as the semantic-to-syntactic bridge. It executes graph queries to traverse the assertions, systematically resolving linked entities. For instance, when querying an \texttt{AgentOSCIN:LLMAgent}, the reader must also traverse the \texttt{AgentOSCIN:agentPrompt} object property to retrieve the role and backstory, and the \texttt{AgentOSCIN:useLanguageModel} property to retrieve the LLM configuration.


\subsubsection{Framework-Specific Code Generation (Layer 3)}
The framework-specific generators are responsible for rendering the SIR into source code. This process accounts for the architectural paradigms of the target framework. For instance, if the target is CrewAI, the generator produces both a Python execution script and the accompanying \texttt{agents.yaml} and \texttt{tasks.yaml} configuration files. If the target is AutoGen, the generator synthesizes a conversational AgentChat implementation, converting the generic agents into explicit \texttt{AssistantAgent} instantiations.

\section{Ontology Evaluation}

To validate whether our AgentOSCIN ontology provides sufficient vocabulary to satisfy the functional and non-functional requirements identified during requirement elicitation, we conducted a systematic evaluation using Competency Questions (CQs) \ref{tab:competency-questions}. These CQs are directly derived from our requirement specifications (see \ref{chap:requirements}).

For each CQ, a corresponding SPARQL query was designed and the evaluation focuses on two primary metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coverage:} Ensuring the ontology contains the necessary properties and classes to return the expected data.
    \item \textbf{Discrimination:} Ensuring that the query results precisely match the logic defined in the ontology without returning irrelevant or incorrect mappings.
\end{itemize}

To provide a robust testing environment, we manually created three populated instances that were based on framework specific examples from the official GitHub repositories of the respective framework . Below, we provide examples of the SPARQL queries: 


\textbf{CQ-1 (Requirement R1: Agent Definition):} Which agents are defined within the system, and what specific attributes identify their roles and types?

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, caption={SPARQL Query for Agent Identification}]
SELECT ?agent ?id ?role ?type ?label WHERE {
    ?agent a agento:LLMAgent .
    OPTIONAL { ?agent agento:agentID   ?id    }
    OPTIONAL { ?agent agento:agentRole  ?role  }
    OPTIONAL { ?agent :agentType        ?type  }
    OPTIONAL { ?agent rdfs:label        ?label }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{CQ-14 (Requirement R14: Coordination):} What is the step-by-step
execution structure of a team’s workflow  and where does the workflow branch conditionally?

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, caption={SPARQL Query for Workflow and Routing}]
SELECT ?pattern ?step ?order ?nextStep ?routingLogic WHERE {
    ?pattern a agento:WorkflowPattern ;
             agento:hasWorkflowStep ?step .
    OPTIONAL { ?step agento:stepOrder ?order }
    OPTIONAL { ?step agento:nextStep  ?nextStep }
    OPTIONAL {
        ?step a :ConditionalStep ;
              :hasRoutingLogic ?routingLogic .
    }
}
ORDER BY ?pattern ?order
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Ontology Evaluation Results}

Running the SPARQL queries over the generated ABoxes shows that AgentOSCIN successfully captures the complex interactions and
definitions required by all three frameworks. Every CQ returns correct
and complete results, giving a Competency Question Coverage of 100\%.

As a comparative baseline, we re-ran the same CQs over the same ABoxes
while restricting the queries to the AgentO vocabulary only. AgentO only answers 13/34 sub-CQs while AgentOSCIN answers all 34. Table~\ref{tab:competency-questions}
reports the per-CQ coverage of both populated ontologies.

The ontology evaluation shows that AgentO alone does not provide sufficient vocabulary to semantically represent agentic AI systems at the level of detail captured by our requirements elicitation. While AgentO works well for describing the overall design of an agentic AI system, it struggles with the finer-grained details required to bridge between heterogeneous frameworks without losing important information. The improvement from 13/34 to 34/34 CQs answered (i.e. from 38.2\% to 100\% coverage) demonstrates that our extension delivers substantial gains over the baseline.

\subsection{Summary of Ontology Evaluation}

The achievement of 100\% concept coverage demonstrates that the ontology possesses a sufficiently expressive vocabulary to capture the structural and behavioral depth of the three analyzed frameworks. Comparative analysis with AgentO further indicates that without the extensions introduced in our work, relevant agentic features cannot be modeled at a depth sufficient to capture the underlying source code logic.  

While these results establish AgentOSCIN as a robust theoretical framework for the framework-agnostic representation of heterogeneous agentic AI implementations, this evaluation does not yet account for its operational usability. To address this, the following section describes a series of controlled transformation experiments conducted to evaluate whether AgentOSCIN can effectively serve as a semantic intermediate layer to enable interoperability between disparate frameworks.


\section{Transformation Experiments}
\label{sec:transformation-experiments}

The transformation experiments utilized the corpus described in \ref{subsec:corpus} and were organized into four primary evaluation tracks designed to measure extraction fidelity, code generation quality, and framework portability.

\paragraph{Intrinsic Extraction Analysis.}
To characterize the structural output of our deterministic pipeline, we first calculate intrinsic metrics on the extracted knowledge graphs without a reference baseline. This analysis measures the size and richness of the produced knowledge through the following indicators:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ABox Triples and Individuals:} The total count of instance-level RDF triples and distinct named individuals, such as agents, tasks, and tools.
    \item \textbf{Properties Used:} The number of unique \texttt{agentoscin} properties exercised, indicating the semantic breadth of the extraction.
    \item \textbf{Density:} The ratio of triples to individuals, serving as a proxy for annotation richness per entity.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Same-Framework Round-Trip and Code Fidelity.}
The Same-Framework Round-Trip experiment ($Source \rightarrow TTL_1 \rightarrow Generate \rightarrow Re\text{-}extract \rightarrow TTL_2$) evaluates how much semantic content survives the full lifecycle of extraction and regeneration. We quantify this preservation using two levels of analysis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ontology Preservation:} We compute pairwise metrics between the initial ($TTL_1$) and re-extracted ($TTL_2$) graphs, specifically Triple F1, Individual F1, and Property F1. We also measure Literal Overlap using Jaccard similarity to ensure textual prompts and descriptions remain intact.
    \item \textbf{Code-Level Fidelity:} We assess the structural accuracy of the generated Python files compared to the ground-truth source code using AST F1, AST Recall, and AST Precision.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Cross-Framework Portability.}
To evaluate framework-agnostic portability, we performed Cross-Framework Round-Trip experiments. This assesses the ontology's ability to act as a neutral intermediary during translation. In addition to standard F1 metrics, we introduce specific measures for cross-framework settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aligned Triple F1:} A metric that tolerates framework-specific URI renames by employing class-aware bipartite alignment of individuals.
    \item \textbf{Fuzzy Avg Score:} The mean similarity of best-matched individual pairs to evaluate naming alignment.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Runtime Behavior and Syntax Validity.}
Finally, we evaluate the operational viability of the generated artifacts. We first verify Syntax Validity by ensuring the generated code parses without errors. While no quantitative runtime performance metric was applied, we systematically documented the manual corrections required to achieve semantic equivalence with the original source, providing a qualitative account of the semantic gaps encountered during execution.
 
\subsection{Corpus}
\label{subsec:corpus}
 
The evaluation corpus consists of twenty projects drawn from the official documentation and example repositories of the three frameworks: seven CrewAI projects, seven AutoGen projects and six LangGraph projects. The projects span common agentic patterns including sequential pipelines, hierarchical research teams, ReAct agents, retrieval-augmented generation, code review, content generation and self-evaluation loops. The corpus deliberately covers heterogeneous orchestration styles to surface framework-specific constructs.

\section{Cross-Framework and Round-Trip Experiments}
\label{sec:roundtrip-evaluation}

The round-trip experiments followed a fixed structure. We conducted same-framework experiments where we transformed framework source code into our semantic representation, generated back the source code, and re-extracted a semantic representation. For each source code example we could then compare both source codes (original and generated) as well as compute similarity metrics between both semantic representations. For each iteration, the metrics were analysed and potential issues were addressed. We iteratively improved the generation and extraction pipeline until we reached metrics we were satisfied with.

\subsubsection{Structural Issues Beyond Import Errors}

Beyond the immediate import and decorator errors, several deeper structural issues affect the usability of the generated code:

\paragraph{Tool implementation stubs.}
The ontology captures tool \emph{signatures} (name, description, parameter types) but not their implementation bodies. Consequently, every generated tool function contains a \texttt{raise NotImplementedError} stub instead of executable logic. Across all same-framework examples, 19~tool functions are generated as stubs; across cross-framework examples, this rises to 46~stubs. While the tool \emph{interface} is correctly preserved (the right tools are bound to the right agents with the correct parameter schemas), the generated code cannot perform actual tool calls without manual implementation.

For example, the original LangGraph \texttt{ReAct} example defines three arithmetic tools (\texttt{add}, \texttt{multiply}, \texttt{subtract}) with single-line implementations. The generated version preserves the tool names, descriptions, and parameter types exactly, but replaces each body with:
\begin{verbatim}
raise NotImplementedError("TODO: implement add")
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{Missing task configurations.}
When translating into CrewAI, the generator must produce YAML configuration files for agents and tasks. While agent configurations (roles, goals, backstories) are well-populated from the ontology's \texttt{Goal} and system message properties, 13~out of 15~cross-framework CrewAI task configuration files are empty (\texttt{\{\}}). This occurs because the source frameworks (AutoGen and LangGraph) do not have an explicit ``task'' concept that maps directly to CrewAI's task abstraction. AutoGen uses implicit turn-taking within group chats, and LangGraph uses graph nodes with arbitrary logic. The ontology's \texttt{Task} class captures task descriptions when present in the source, but these are often absent or expressed as inline prompt strings rather than structured task definitions.

\paragraph{CrewAI Flow state leakage.}

When LangGraph examples are translated to CrewAI, the generated \texttt{Flow} state class inherits LangGraph-specific type annotations. For instance, the \texttt{ReAct} agent's state definition \texttt{messages: Annotated[Sequence[BaseMessage], add\_messages]} appears verbatim in the CrewAI output, despite \texttt{Annotated}, \texttt{Sequence}, \texttt{BaseMessage}, and \texttt{add\_messages} being LangGraph/LangChain constructs with no CrewAI equivalent. This indicates that the ontology preserves state schema information at too low a level of abstraction---it stores the raw type annotation strings rather than mapping them to framework-agnostic state concepts.

\subsection{Summary of the Deterministic Approach}
\label{subsec:deterministic-ceiling}

The results above represent the outcome of multiple iterations of pipeline refinement. In early iterations, Triple~F1 values were significantly lower (e.g., below~0.7 for several LangGraph examples), typically due to missing extraction rules, incomplete code generation templates, or edge cases in how framework-specific patterns map to the ontology.

With each iteration, we identified the sources of semantic loss (e.g. missing tool bindings, incomplete flow step extraction, dropped configuration parameters) and added corresponding extraction rules or generation templates into our determinsitic transformation pipeline. However, we observed diminishing returns: after approximately five iterations, the remaining discrepancies were increasingly framework-specific edge cases where adding more rules would mean hard-coding framework version-specific behaviour rather than capturing generalisable patterns.

We therefore accepted the current metrics as the deterministic ceiling where the point at which further rule-based improvements would sacrifice generality and risk overfitting to the specific examples in our evaluation set. The overall Triple~F1 of~0.929 and AST~F1 of~0.904 indicate that the deterministic pipeline preserves the large majority of both semantic and structural information through the same-framework round-trip while the Aligned F1 of 0.584 in the cross-framework round-trip indicated further improvement possibilities. 


\subsection{Hybrid Refinement and Runtime Operationality}

While the deterministic pipeline demonstrates high structural fidelity—achieving a mean Triple F1 of 0.824 across all frameworks \cite{cite_1}---purely deterministic approaches often struggle with runtime-specific implementation details. To mitigate issues such as concrete tool implementation, complex routing logic, and runtime bugs introduced during cross-framework translation, a hybrid baseline was developed.

This approach utilizes the deterministically generated source code and the corresponding AgentOSCIN ABox as context for a LLM refinement step. Using the OpenAI API, the system was prompted to improve the code based on typical error patterns identified during runtime analysis. 

The results of this hybrid approach were mixed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LangGraph:} Observed minor improvements in runtime stability, particularly regarding state management within nodes.
    \item \textbf{CrewAI:} The hybrid model struggled with the recommended multi-file flow structure \cite{crewai_docs_2025}, often introducing new structural issues rather than resolving implementation gaps.
    \item \textbf{AutoGen:} Consistent with previous findings, the deterministic output already maintained 100\% syntax validity and required no further refinement for basic execution.
\end{itemize}

Notably, AST F1 metrics showed no significant improvement, as the LLM's refinements focused on implementation details that do not alter the high-level structural features captured by the AST \ref{sec:transformation-experiments}. Similarly, re-extracted ABox metrics remained stagnant, suggesting that the "improved" code did not add or recover additional semantic content beyond the original deterministic extraction.

\section{Interpretation of Findings}

The results of this thesis demonstrate that a semantic representation can close the gap between the source code of different agentic AI frameworks. While the cross-framework metrics show moderate performance in terms of exact triple preservation (Triple~F1~=~0.352), the Alignedi~F1 of ~0.584 and high Property~F1 of~0.812 Individual~F1 of~0.759 indicate that the overall structural vocabulary and the entities themselves transfer well across frameworks.

It is important to note what the runtime evaluation does and does not tell us. The runtime pass rate only tests whether the generated source code can be loaded and initialized without raising an error. It does not verify that the generated system behaves correctly, that agents interact as intended, or that the system produces the same outputs as the original. In that sense, a passing runtime check is closer to confirming that the code compiles than to confirming that the system works. With this limitation in mind, the runtime results still provide a useful signal about the structural soundness of the generated code.

AutoGen works well for both same-framework and cross-framework generation because its architecture is flat and declarative at the API level: agents are instantiated as objects, tools are wrapped in constructors, and teams are assembled via a single call. There is very little routing logic or imperative control flow that would need to be reconstructed. This means the generator can produce structurally complete and loadable code for AutoGen targets with minimal information loss.

For CrewAI and LangGraph, the picture is more nuanced and depends heavily on the nature of the original source code. The more logic was coded into Flows (CrewAI) or StateGraphs (LangGraph), the more problems the generator had producing working source code. Simple examples with straightforward agent-task structures generated well, while examples with conditional routing, iterative loops, or complex state management required manual adjustments before they could run. These manual adjustments included implementing tool function bodies (which the ontology captures as signatures but not implementations), fixing missing or incorrect imports, adding decorator arguments that the generator omitted, and resolving framework-specific type annotations that leaked across framework boundaries. The evaluation chapter (Section~\ref{subsec:runtime-behaviour}) reports these issues in detail.

The deterministic extraction proved to be a clear success, offering substantially higher fidelity and reliability than the LLM baseline. Against hand-authored gold-standard ABoxes, the deterministic pipeline achieved a mean Triple~F1 of~0.992 and a mean Aligned~F1 of~0.992 across all six evaluated systems, while the LLM baseline did not exceed a Triple~F1 of~0.000 on any system and achieved a mean Aligned~F1 of only~0.022. While improvements to the LLM prompts could be explored in future work, the issues identified, namely 44\% parse failures, structural hallucination, and re-extraction errors, reflect problems previously documented in the literature regarding the non-deterministic and hallucinatory nature of LLM-based transformation approaches.

The deterministic pipeline also proves to be better suited for transformation than the LLM approach, as it allows for explicit cross-framework mappings that preserve information an LLM would not be able to interpret without detailed knowledge of these mappings. Encoding such knowledge into a prompt would bloat it substantially and could introduce further problems. This advantage is especially visible in the gold evaluation, where the deterministic pipeline produces more properties than the hand-authored ground truth per system, because the pipeline is designed to infer additional semantic information for better cross-framework usability. The gold evaluation, comparing manually created ground truth TTL files for two selected examples per framework against both the deterministic pipeline and the LLM baseline, demonstrates this clearly: the deterministic pipeline achieves a mean Triple~F1 above~0.99, while the LLM baseline does not rise above~0.00 on any individual system.

The results of the cross-framework experiments revealed expected behaviour patterns across the different frameworks. AutoGen's agent-chat architecture proves to be the easiest to generate to, as this framework has the least framework-specific routing logic compared to, for example, LangGraph's state graphs and CrewAI's Flow decorators. The generated AutoGen code closely mirrors the original structure across both same-framework and cross-framework experiments, producing very similar and loadable code in both directions.

The other two frameworks showed more information loss during the transformation experiments. While LangGraph-to-CrewAI translations achieve a relatively high Triple~F1 of~0.731, indicating that the flow and graph structures can be successfully mapped to one another, both frameworks encode more logic imperatively in their source code. This leads to the overall structure being well aligned (Property~F1 around 0.8 to 0.9) but the generated code often requiring manual adjustment before it can be executed, particularly for examples with complex control flow.

Another important finding was that CrewAI has the most declarative structure, making it relatively easy to extract from and generate well-populated source code. Its YAML-based configuration for agents and tasks maps directly onto the ontology, which is reflected in CrewAI achieving the highest same-framework Triple~F1 (0.966). LangGraph, by contrast, has no real declarative structure where much of the information needs to be inferred from imperative graph-building calls such as \texttt{add\_node} and \texttt{add\_edge}, which is reflected in its lower same-framework Triple~F1 (0.935). AutoGen sits in an interesting middle ground: while its AST~F1 is the highest (0.984), its Triple~F1 is the lowest among same-framework results (0.885), indicating that the generated Python closely mirrors the original but some semantic information around complex team compositions is restructured during the round-trip.

The hybrid approach, that is extending the deterministically generated source code through an LLM, showed only marginal improvement. There was no way to reliably improve the source code across all frameworks. While most of the structural bugs were removed or at least minimised depending on the model used, the LLM produced semantically incorrect source code in several cases. For CrewAI, the LLM was still trained on the older process-based structure and not on the current Flow-based architecture, leading to semantically wrong output. LangGraph examples were structurally and semantically the most correct and showed the best improvement over the solely deterministic approach, partly because the single-file structure and the descriptions provided the LLM with enough context to move from non-executable to executable source code. The AutoGen examples were mostly structurally and semantically correct already, leading to the least improvement over the deterministic approach.

Regarding the ontology, the competency question coverage showed that we notably improved the semantic richness needed to use the ontology as a framework-agnostic representation. AgentO answered only 13 of 34 sub-CQs (38.2\%), while AgentOSCIN answers all 34 (100\%). The overall positive metrics regarding the similarity of original and generated code, combined with the substantial improvement over the LLM baseline, show that AgentOSCIN provides sufficient semantic expressiveness to enable interoperability between frameworks. While the issues described above still show there is room for improvement, we demonstrated how a semantic representation can bridge the gap identified in the problem statement, namely the absence of a shared semantic representation with enough vocabulary to mitigate the monolithic development practices, framework-specific and tightly coupled abstractions, and hard-coded architectural decisions that characterise current agentic AI systems.

Beyond demonstrating this through the transformation experiments and the competency coverage over existing representations, the transformation pipeline and its results can serve as the basis for further studies regarding ontologies for source code interoperability and the application perspective of generating source code from ontologies. While this was not the main goal of the thesis, the transformation pipeline demonstrated usability and a significant improvement over the baseline.


\section{Threats to Validity}

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
The gold-standard ABoxes were authored by the thesis author and subsequently verified against the pipeline output. This creates a risk of confirmation bias, as discrepancies were resolved in favour of the source code semantics, but subtle interpretation differences may remain. Additionally, the iterative refinement of extraction rules and generation templates was guided by the same corpus used for evaluation, which may lead to overfitting to the specific examples. We mitigated this by accepting a deterministic ceiling (Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-ceiling}) rather than adding rules for every edge case.

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
The metrics used, namely Triple~F1, AST~F1, and runtime pass rate, each capture a different facet of transformation quality, but none is a complete measure of semantic equivalence. Triple~F1 treats all triples equally, though some carry more semantic weight than others. For example, an agent's tool binding is arguably more important than its display label. The runtime pass rate is particularly limited in what it measures: it only tests whether the generated code can be loaded and initialised without raising an error, which is closer to a compilation check than a correctness check. A system that passes this test may still contain incorrect agent interactions, wrong tool bindings, or produce entirely different outputs than the original. We did not conduct behavioural equivalence testing, which means the runtime numbers should not be read as a measure of how well the generated systems actually work.

\paragraph{External validity.}
The evaluation corpus of 20~examples was drawn from official framework repositories and documentation, which may not represent the full diversity of real-world agentic systems. Production systems are likely to be larger, use more complex orchestration patterns, and rely on custom abstractions not covered by the three frameworks studied. The three frameworks themselves, CrewAI, AutoGen, and LangGraph, were chosen for their popularity and architectural diversity, but they do not cover the entire landscape of agentic AI frameworks such as DSPy, Semantic Kernel, or proprietary solutions.

\paragraph{Reliability.}
The LLM baseline and hybrid approach results depend on the specific model used (GPT-4o) and its behaviour at the time of evaluation. LLM outputs are inherently non-deterministic, and results may differ with different models, prompting strategies, or even different runs of the same model. We ran a single evaluation pass and repeated runs with confidence intervals would strengthen the findings. The deterministic pipeline, by contrast, is fully reproducible.


\section{Relation to Research Questions}

\paragraph{RQ1: Which architectural constructs must a semantic representation support to capture agentic AI systems implemented in CrewAI, AutoGen, and LangGraph?}

The requirement elicitation identified 21~representational requirements spanning agents, tasks, orchestration, workflow control flow, prompts, tools, memory, guardrails, and termination conditions. These requirements were formalised as 21~competency questions with 34~sub-questions. AgentOSCIN answers all~34 sub-CQs, compared to 13~for the base AgentO ontology. The ontology extension adds 9~top-level classes and 11~subclasses, 17~object properties, and 28~datatype properties. The vocabulary coverage evaluation shows that the combined corpus exercises 25~of~48 classes and 60~of~98 properties, with framework-specific constructs such as \texttt{hasGuardrail} for CrewAI, \texttt{hasMaxTurns} for AutoGen, and \texttt{hasRoutingLogic} for LangGraph appearing exclusively in their respective frameworks' outputs.

\paragraph{RQ2: To what extent can semantic representations of agentic AI systems be systematically derived from their source code?}

The deterministic AST-based extraction pipeline successfully processes all~20 examples in the corpus, producing valid TTL files with ABox sizes ranging from~69 to~196 triples. Against hand-authored gold-standard ABoxes, the pipeline achieves a mean Triple~F1 of~0.992, mean Individual~F1 of~0.998, and mean Property~F1 of~0.998 across six evaluated systems. The same-framework round-trip further confirms extraction quality with an overall Triple~F1 of~0.929 and AST~F1 of~0.904. These results show that semantic representations can be derived from source code with high fidelity using a deterministic approach. The LLM baseline, by contrast, achieves very low triple and aligned F1 scored (0 - 0.5), which can be attributed to missing context and hallucinations by the LLM model used for transformation. 

\paragraph{RQ3: To what extent can the source code of agentic AI systems be generated from their semantic representations?}

The generation results show a clear gradient. Same-framework generation preserves the structural vocabulary well (Property~F1~=~0.976, AST~F1~=~0.904). Cross-framework generation maintains high structural alignment (Property~F1~=~0.812, Individual~F1~=~0.759) but with lower instance-level fidelity (Triple~F1~=~0.352). The results demonstrate that source code can be generated from semantic representations to a degree that preserves the architectural structure of the original system, though framework-specific idioms and imperative logic remain challenging. AutoGen targets produce structurally complete code in both same-framework and cross-framework settings due to its flat API, while CrewAI and LangGraph targets require varying degrees of manual adjustment depending on how much control flow logic was present in the original source. The runtime evaluation, which tests only whether the code loads without error and not whether it behaves correctly, confirms this pattern but should be interpreted with caution given its limited scope (see Section~\ref{subsec:runtime-behaviour}).

\paragraph{RQ4: Which methods can be used to adequately evaluate the quality of extraction and generation of agentic AI systems from framework-specific source code?}

This thesis employed a multi-faceted evaluation methodology combining competency question coverage for ontology validation, gold-standard comparison using hand-authored ABoxes for extraction accuracy, round-trip semantic metrics (Triple~F1, Aligned~F1, Property~F1, Individual~F1, Literal Overlap) for end-to-end fidelity, AST~F1 for structural source code comparison, runtime pass rate for executability, and an LLM-only baseline for contextualising the deterministic pipeline. The combination of these methods proved necessary, as no single metric captures all aspects of transformation quality. Triple~F1 misses structural equivalences that Aligned~F1 captures, AST~F1 can be high while Triple~F1 is low as seen with AutoGen, and runtime pass rate reveals practical issues invisible to all semantic metrics. The gold evaluation additionally showed that round-trip metrics, while useful, can mask extraction errors that cancel out during re-extraction. Direct comparison against ground truth provides a more rigorous assessment.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}
% =============================================================

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This thesis investigated the extent to which semantic representations can enable interoperability between agentic AI frameworks. The work makes the following contributions:

First, we extended the AgentO ontology into AgentOSCIN, adding 20~classes and 45~properties to support the full range of constructs found in CrewAI, AutoGen, and LangGraph. The extension raises competency question coverage from 38.2\% (13/34) to 100\% (34/34), demonstrating that the vocabulary is rich enough to serve as a framework-agnostic intermediate representation.

Second, we developed an AST-based extraction pipeline that lifts framework-specific source code into AgentOSCIN ABoxes with a mean Triple~F1 of~0.992 against hand-authored gold standards. The pipeline processes all~20 examples in the evaluation corpus without failure.

Third, we developed template-based code generators for all three frameworks that produce source code from AgentOSCIN ABoxes, achieving a same-framework AST~F1 of~0.904. The generated code is structurally complete for frameworks with flat APIs such as AutoGen, while frameworks with more imperative control flow such as CrewAI Flows and LangGraph StateGraphs require varying degrees of manual adjustment.

Fourth, we established a multi-metric evaluation framework combining gold-standard comparison, round-trip semantic metrics, AST-level source code comparison, runtime executability testing, and an LLM baseline. The gold evaluation with six hand-authored ABoxes provides a reusable benchmark for future work.

Fifth, the round-trip experiments across three frameworks and 50~cross-framework translation pairs provide empirical evidence that a shared semantic representation can preserve the architectural structure of agentic systems across framework boundaries. 


\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Several limitations constrain the generalisability of the results presented in this thesis. The evaluation corpus of 20~examples, while diverse in orchestration patterns, consists of relatively small systems. Production agentic systems with dozens of agents, complex state management, and custom orchestration logic may expose additional gaps in the ontology or the extraction pipeline. The three frameworks studied represent only a subset of the agentic AI landscape, and extending the approach to frameworks such as DSPy, Semantic Kernel, or AutoGPT would test the ontology's claim to generality.

The hybrid approach showed only marginal improvement over the deterministic pipeline, partly due to the LLM being trained on outdated framework structures such as CrewAI's older process-based architecture rather than its current Flow-based design. For further iterations of the hybrid approach, there need to be additional experiments regarding which LLMs to use and how prompts can be improved to address the identified issues. With the rapid development of AI and agentic AI, there also seem to be possibilities of using the ontology and the deterministic approach as a tool within an agentic system developed solely for framework transformation.

The runtime pass rate, while a useful practical metric, only tests whether the code loads without error. It does not verify behavioural correctness, that is whether the generated system actually produces the same outputs or exhibits the same agent interactions as the original. Developing behavioural equivalence tests would strengthen the evaluation considerably.

If future research finds ways to reliably generate and extract framework source code for agentic AI systems, this could be used to mitigate the interoperability challenges at scale. It would not matter in what framework a system is built if it can be reliably translated and transformed into any other. In such a scenario, AgentOSCIN, if adopted as the intermediate layer, could serve as the shared semantic backbone enabling this translation. The transformation pipeline and evaluation methodology developed in this thesis provide a foundation for pursuing this vision.
